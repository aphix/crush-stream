> Question: How much force or energy pops a modern car's latched trunk lid or tailgate in a rear-end collision (FMVSS 206 / ECE R11 latch loads), and at what rear-impact speed do lids typically spring open?

A modern trunk latch is generally designed to resist **several kilonewtons of direct separating load**, not a single universal “pop-open” force. The applicable U.S. benchmark is roughly **9–11 kN in the fully latched position** and **4.5 kN in the secondary position**, depending on load direction and test configuration.[3][4]

## Relevant latch-strength numbers

| Requirement or example | Load |
|---|---:|
| FMVSS 206 fully latched, longitudinal test | **11,000 N** |
| FMVSS 206 fully latched, alternate longitudinal test | **9,000 N** |
| FMVSS 206 fully latched, transverse test | **9,000 N** |
| FMVSS 206 secondary latch | **4,500 N** |
| ECE R11 fully latched, longitudinal | approximately **11,110 N** |
| ECE R11 fully latched, transverse | approximately **8,890 N** |
| ECE R11 secondary latch | approximately **4,440 N** |

The NHTSA FMVSS 206 test procedure explicitly lists **11,000 N fully latched** and **4,500 N secondary-latched** loads; it also specifies **9,000 N** fully-latched tests and applies back-door/liftgate test provisions.[3] Production latch compliance tests commonly show applied loads above the minimum—for example, about **13.9 kN** in a fully latched test and **7.1 kN** in a secondary-latch test.[6]

These are **static tensile or fixture loads**, not the energy required to open a lid in a crash. A force rating does not directly convert to joules without knowing the latch displacement and the structure’s deformation. As a rough mechanical scale, applying \(10{,}000\ \text{N}\) through \(10\ \text{mm}\) of effective deformation corresponds to about:

\[
E \approx Fd = 10{,}000 \times 0.010 \approx 100\ \text{J}
\]

Actual crash energy absorbed by the latch and surrounding sheet metal may be much higher or lower because the load path, deformation distance, latch geometry, striker movement, hinges, and seal compression matter.

## Can a rear impact “pop” the trunk open?

There is **no general rear-impact speed at which modern trunk lids typically open**. A properly latched lid should remain closed in ordinary rear crashes, and opening depends more on:

- Rear-end intrusion or buckling near the latch and striker
- Relative movement between the body and deck lid
- Latch misalignment or pre-existing damage
- Whether the lid was fully engaged in the primary detent
- Damage to the latch release cable, actuator, wiring, or hinges
- Liftgate mass and gas-strut forces
- Impact direction and pulse duration

FMVSS 206 is a **static-strength regulation**, not a crash-speed threshold. It therefore cannot be used to say that a trunk will open at, for example, 20 or 30 km/h.

NHTSA’s compliance procedure includes a separate automatic-opening check for certain trunk-release systems, conducted at **5 km/h or less**, but that is a low-speed release-function test—not evidence that a collision at 5 km/h causes a trunk to open.[1]

## Practical interpretation

- **Latch-only failure:** roughly **4.5–11 kN** of unfavorable load is the relevant regulatory scale.
- **Whole-lid opening:** usually requires latch/striker misalignment, local sheet-metal deformation, or release-system actuation; it is not determined by vehicle speed alone.
- **Crash speed:** no defensible universal threshold exists. A severe, concentrated low-speed impact can open a lid, while a higher-speed crash with favorable load paths may leave it closed.
- **Real-accident reports:** they are not suitable for deriving a typical opening speed because impact overlap, vehicle compatibility, braking, and pre-impact latch condition vary widely.

Thus, the best engineering answer is **“several kilonewtons, with approximately 9–11 kN primary and 4.5 kN secondary regulatory load levels—not a specific km/h threshold.”**
