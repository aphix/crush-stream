> Question: In Burnout 3: Takedown, Burnout Revenge and Burnout Paradise (and Criterion's NFS Hot Pursuit 2010 / Most Wanted 2012), how do the AI rivals or cops attack the player and how are 'takedowns' and aftertouch designed? Concrete specifics: how AI chooses to slam or shunt, rubber-banding, rival aggression scaling, revenge/rival memory systems (Burnout Revenge rivals remembering who took them down), the crash-camera and takedown reward loop, and why it felt satisfying. Cite developer interviews and GDC talks where possible.

Criterion’s racers are best understood as **designed antagonists**, not simulations of fair racing. They are tuned to keep the player in contact, create opportunities for spectacular contact, and convert both success and failure into another decision: attack, escape, retaliate, or redirect a wreck.

## Burnout 3: Takedown

AI rivals primarily attack through **side-on shunts and rear-quarter slams**. A rival that is alongside the player can steer across the player’s line, while one behind can use speed and boost to hit the rear or rear corner. The result is intentionally readable: being near a wall, traffic, or another rival makes the player vulnerable, but also creates a chance to counterattack.

The game’s catch-up system prevents a clean lead from becoming a foregone conclusion. Rivals remain close enough to threaten the player, while the player’s own mistakes—especially crashes—produce large position swings. This is rubber-banding as **dramatic pacing**, though it is restrained enough that skilled driving can still create meaningful leads; later comparisons often describe Burnout 3’s catch-up as less oppressive than Revenge’s.[1][6]

A normal takedown is therefore more than a collision. The player must combine:

- **Relative speed:** boosting into a rival gives the impact enough force.
- **Attack angle:** a rear-quarter or side impact is more effective than a weak head-on touch.
- **Environment:** walls, barriers, traffic, and oncoming lanes become weapons.
- **Timing:** the best attack is often made while the rival is already committed to a corner or obstruction.

The reward loop makes the physical attack immediately valuable. A takedown fills and extends boost, while the defeated rival is temporarily removed from the race.[1] The crash camera then pauses or slows the action and gives visual priority to the wreck. That camera is not merely presentation: it confirms causality—**your maneuver caused that spectacular result**—and briefly turns a dangerous race into a moment of recognition.

The satisfaction comes from the unusually short distance between input and reward:

1. Spot a rival near danger.
2. Choose an angle and commit.
3. Watch the rival enter a violent crash sequence.
4. Receive boost, score, and positional relief.
5. Re-enter the race with momentum.

Burnout 3 also lets the player turn failure into offense. After being taken down, the wreck can enter an impact-time state in which the player steers the damaged car toward traffic or another rival; connecting produces an **aftertouch takedown**.[1] This creates a “revenge fantasy” without requiring the player to restart or simply accept a lost position.

## Burnout Revenge

Revenge makes the attack system more explicit and personal. Rivals that take the player down are marked as **Revenge rivals**, commonly represented with a red indicator. Eliminating that rival yields a larger boost reward than an ordinary takedown.[3][10]

That marker functions as a lightweight memory system rather than a complex personality model. The game remembers the immediate offender, identifies them visually, and turns the next encounter into a priority target. The player is no longer merely looking for “a car to hit”; they are looking for **the car that just ruined their race**.

Revenge’s AI is correspondingly more aggressive. Contemporary coverage described opponents as smarter and “extremely aggressive,” while also noting that they were given “lessons in fairness”—a useful description of Criterion’s design goal: rivals should attack hard, but attacks should remain legible and exploitable.[10] In practice, they:

- Stay close enough to initiate side shunts.
- Use rear impacts when following.
- Exploit traffic and barriers.
- Attempt to keep the player in the middle of a dense pack.
- Become especially dangerous when the player is already committed to a narrow line.

The game’s comeback behavior is stronger than Burnout 3’s. Opponents are deliberately kept within reach, and reviews frequently describe the AI as maintaining a tight pack through catch-up behavior.[1][8][10] That produces constant pressure, but it also means that a crash can feel disproportionately costly: one failed corner may drop the player from the front to the back.

Revenge also expands the vocabulary of attacks:

- **Wall takedown:** shunt or ram a rival into a wall or structure.
- **Traffic takedown:** push traffic into a rival or use a checked vehicle as a projectile.
- **Aftertouch takedown:** steer the wreck after being eliminated into an opponent.
- **Psyche-out:** evade or bait a rival’s attack so that the rival crashes instead.[1][3]

The change from Burnout 3 is significant. Aftertouch is easier to exploit because rivals are less successful at avoiding the player’s wreck, while ordinary takedowns can feel less consistent because of altered collision tuning and the greater emphasis on traffic interactions.[10] That trade-off shifts the game toward **managing a moving hazard field**, not just lining up clean direct hits.

The Revenge reward loop adds escalation:

- A takedown provides boost and removes a threat.
- A Revenge takedown provides an enhanced reward and emotional closure.
- Repeated revenge takedowns increase recognition and reinforce target selection.[10]

This is why the red-rival system feels memorable despite being mechanically simple: it supplies a narrative state—**unfinished business**—inside a race that otherwise has no dialogue or character development.

## Burnout Paradise

Paradise changes the context more than the basic collision fantasy. The open world removes the tightly scripted event flow of the earlier games, so opponents and traffic must create excitement through encounters that emerge during free driving and event routes.

The core Burnout logic remains: rivals are most threatening when they can exploit the player’s vulnerability near traffic, walls, junctions, and other vehicles. But Paradise’s open structure makes the player’s own route choice more important. A player can seek riskier shortcuts and denser traffic for faster progress, while the same environments provide the materials for takedowns.

The principal difference is that Paradise’s spectacle is less dependent on a dedicated takedown-camera reward than Burnout 3 and Revenge. The emphasis moves toward continuous world flow: launching an event, finding routes, drifting through intersections, and improvising attacks without the race stopping for a heavily staged confirmation.

## Criterion’s Need for Speed: Hot Pursuit (2010)

Hot Pursuit translates the same contact-driven thinking into a **cop-versus-racer combat system**.

Cops attack with pursuit tools and interception rather than Burnout-style takedown scoring alone. Their objective is to force a racer into a crash, immobilize them, or deny their route. Racers, meanwhile, use their own countermeasures to break pursuit and disrupt police units. This creates an asymmetric combat loop:

- Cops pressure, box in, and intercept.
- Racers evade, counter, and create separation.
- Both sides use the road network as a tactical space.
- A crash is valuable because it changes pursuit geometry, not merely because it looks spectacular.

The Burnout inheritance is clearest in the dramatic physicality of police contact and the emphasis on repeated encounters rather than clean simulation. However, Hot Pursuit generally presents the conflict as a pursuit battle rather than using Burnout 3’s signature takedown camera as the central reward.

## Need for Speed: Most Wanted (2012)

Most Wanted retains Criterion’s aggressive traffic-and-contact philosophy, but it is often criticized for more conspicuous rubber-banding. Reviews describe opponents that keep the race tight and can appear to recover implausibly after falling behind.[2][7] That system helps preserve tension during long open-road events, but it can also weaken the player’s sense that superior driving has permanently earned a lead.

Most Wanted also omits Burnout’s famous takedown camera, a change explicitly noted by contemporary criticism.[2] Without that camera, a successful crash has less of a staged “payoff beat.” The player still experiences impact, pursuit, and wreckage, but the game gives less ceremonial emphasis to the moment when an opponent is eliminated.

That difference matters because the takedown camera performed three jobs:

- **Feedback:** it made clear which rival had been defeated.
- **Reward:** it transformed a collision into a short spectacle.
- **Authorship:** it framed the event as the player’s intentional achievement.

Removing it makes the action faster and arguably better suited to open-world flow, but it also removes part of the emotional punctuation that made Burnout’s takedowns so satisfying.

## Why the system felt satisfying

Criterion’s design combines four forms of reinforcement:

1. **Physical readability:** the player can usually understand why a rival crashed—wall, traffic, angle, speed, or failed attack.
2. **Immediate audiovisual confirmation:** impact effects, slow motion, camera emphasis, and wreck animations make the result unambiguous.
3. **Mechanical profit:** takedowns refill or extend boost, helping the player continue attacking rather than merely stopping to celebrate.[1]
4. **Emotional targeting:** Revenge’s marked rival turns an abstract race position into a remembered grievance.[3][10]

The result is a rare loop in which aggression is both strategically useful and emotionally legible. The AI is not simply cheating to challenge the player; it is repeatedly offering a dangerous interaction. When the rival attacks successfully, the player gets a clear target and an aftertouch opportunity. When the player attacks successfully, the game grants speed, spectacle, and temporary control of the race.

That is the central Criterion trick: **failure remains playable, success remains violent, and both states generate another chance to make a dramatic decision.**
