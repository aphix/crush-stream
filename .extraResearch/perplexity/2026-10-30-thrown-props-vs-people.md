> Question: What change in a standing adult's centre-of-mass velocity (m/s), or what impulse (N·s), from an unexpected push or impact at the chest makes a fall likely? Cite push-recovery or balance studies (Hof's extrapolated centre of mass, perturbation-platform or pull/push studies). Also, what coefficient of restitution does a rigid object (a wooden crate, a plastic traffic cone) have when it strikes a human torso, i.e. does it mostly stick or bounce, so the transferred impulse is m·v or up to (1+e)·m·v?

There is **no single chest-push velocity or impulse that reliably predicts a fall**. The threshold depends strongly on stance width, foot position, friction, body mass, age, reaction time, whether a step is permitted, and the height and direction of the applied force.

## Practical biomechanical threshold

Hof’s extrapolated center of mass is

\[
x_{\mathrm{XCoM}}=x_{\mathrm{COM}}+\frac{v_{\mathrm{COM}}}{\omega_0},
\qquad
\omega_0=\sqrt{\frac{g}{l}},
\]

where \(l\) is the effective pendulum length. A person is at the **instantaneous limit of balance** when the XCoM reaches the edge of the base of support; beyond that, a step or other recovery action is required.[6]

For a typical standing adult with \(l\approx 1\ \mathrm{m}\),

\[
\omega_0\approx 3.13\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}.
\]

Thus, if the center of mass starts near the middle of a stance whose available forward margin is about \(0.10\)–\(0.20\ \mathrm{m}\), the corresponding COM-speed scale is roughly

\[
v_{\mathrm{COM}}\approx \omega_0 d
\approx 0.31\text{–}0.63\ \mathrm{m/s}.
\]

That is a **boundary-of-balance estimate**, not a guaranteed fall threshold. A forward velocity around **0.3 m/s** may consume the available margin in a narrow or unstable stance; **0.5–0.6 m/s** is more likely to require a step or produce loss of balance if no effective step occurs. With a rapid protective step, substantially larger perturbations can often be recovered.

The equivalent whole-body impulse is approximately

\[
J=M\,\Delta v_{\mathrm{COM}}.
\]

For an 80-kg adult:

- \(0.30\ \mathrm{m/s}\rightarrow 24\ \mathrm{N\,s}\)
- \(0.50\ \mathrm{m/s}\rightarrow 40\ \mathrm{N\,s}\)
- \(0.60\ \mathrm{m/s}\rightarrow 48\ \mathrm{N\,s}\)

These figures describe the impulse actually delivered to the body’s COM, not simply the momentum of an impacting object. A chest impact can produce rotation, local deformation, and arm motion, so the COM impulse may be much smaller than the object’s nominal momentum.

## What perturbation studies imply

Hof’s framework treats balance as maintaining the extrapolated COM within the base of support; when the XCoM moves outside it, a compensatory step is needed to prevent a fall.[6] Perturbation-platform and pull/push experiments therefore usually report outcomes as recovery steps, stepping thresholds, or falls—not as one universal COM velocity.

A useful interpretation is:

- **Below roughly 0.2–0.3 m/s COM velocity:** many healthy adults can recover without a large step, depending on stance.
- **Around 0.3–0.6 m/s:** the person is commonly in a step-recovery zone; a delayed, obstructed, or absent step can lead to a fall.
- **Above roughly 0.6 m/s:** recovery becomes increasingly dependent on a rapid, well-placed step and available space.

Those ranges should not be treated as injury or safety cutoffs. Older adults, people with narrow stance or reduced reaction capacity, and subjects whose feet are constrained can fall at lower perturbations.

## Does a crate or cone bounce off the torso?

There is **no generally valid measured coefficient of restitution for “a wooden crate versus a human torso” or “a plastic traffic cone versus a torso.”** The coefficient depends on impact speed, contact geometry, orientation, padding, clothing, body posture, and whether the object deforms or slides. A coefficient of restitution of \(e=0\) represents a perfectly plastic, non-bouncing collision, while \(e=1\) represents a perfectly elastic one.[2][9]

For a rigid object striking a compliant, deformable torso, the effective \(e\) is usually expected to be **low**, because energy is dissipated in soft tissue, clothing, object deformation, friction, and body motion. The object generally does not rebound as though it struck a rigid wall. In many practical chest impacts, “mostly sticks during the contact” is a better approximation than “bounces,” but assigning a numerical \(e\) without experiment would be unjustified.

The impulse is governed by the change in momentum:

\[
J = m_{\mathrm{object}}(v_{\mathrm{in}}-v_{\mathrm{out}}).
\]

If the object approaches at speed \(v\) and rebounds with speed \(e v\), then the idealized one-dimensional result is

\[
J=m_{\mathrm{object}}(1+e)v.
\]

Therefore:

- **No rebound, \(e\approx0\):** \(J\approx mv\)
- **Small rebound, \(e\approx0.1\text{–}0.3\):** \(J\approx1.1\text{–}1.3\,mv\)
- **Rigid-wall-like rebound, \(e\approx1\):** \(J\approx2mv\)

For a human torso, the first or second case is generally more defensible than assuming \(2mv\). However, the force can still be large because

\[
F_{\mathrm{average}}\approx \frac{J}{\Delta t},
\]

and a short contact time produces a high peak force even when the object does not bounce.

A further complication is that the torso is not fixed: if the person moves backward, part of the object’s momentum becomes motion of the person, while some is absorbed by deformation and rotation. Consequently, \(m v\) is a reasonable lower-order estimate for a non-rebounding impact, but the actual COM velocity change requires measuring the coupled object–body dynamics rather than selecting a universal torso restitution coefficient.

> Follow-up question: How fast can an adult throw a 4 kg traffic cone, a 30 kg wooden crate, a ~60 kg stack of car tyres and a ~180 kg small hay bale (release speed, m/s)? Cite studies of thrown load or shot-put/hammer/medicine-ball release speed versus implement mass, and say how release speed scales with mass (roughly constant energy, constant impulse, or other).

A reasonable **adult one-off throw estimate** is:

| Object mass | Plausible release speed | Approximate kinetic energy |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 4 kg traffic cone | **8–12 m/s** | 130–290 J |
| 30 kg wooden crate | **2.5–4 m/s** | 95–240 J |
| ~60 kg stack of tyres | **1.5–2.5 m/s** | 70–190 J |
| ~180 kg small hay bale | **0.7–1.3 m/s** | 45–150 J |

These are broad estimates, not experimentally validated predictions: object shape, grip, lifting height, stance, training, and whether the throw is overhead, forward, or a shove can change the result substantially. A 180 kg bale may be impossible to accelerate into a clean airborne throw for many adults; the quoted range assumes a strong adult can get it moving and release it.

## What the evidence suggests

The available studies support a decline in speed with increasing mass, but **not a single universal scaling law**:

- In a seated single-arm medicine-ball shot-put study using 1, 2, and 3 kg balls, dominant-arm release velocity averaged about **5.15 m/s**, with ball mass affecting projectile performance.[2]
- A study using 1–3 kg medicine balls found that increasing mass raised release momentum by roughly **34–35%**, implying that speed decreased much less steeply than \(1/m\) over that range.[1]
- Another medicine-ball study reported a broadly linear force–velocity relationship across several loads, consistent with a muscle system whose attainable velocity falls as external load rises.[3]
- A review/model of shot-put and hammer-throw data found that throw distance is approximately inversely related to implement mass.[4] Since, for similar release angles and heights, range is roughly proportional to \(v^2\), this corresponds approximately to
  \[
  v \propto m^{-1/2}.
  \]

For comparison, elite hammer throwers release a **7.26 kg men’s hammer** at high speed; the hammer’s release velocity is a primary determinant of distance.[9] That does not transfer directly to an untrained adult throwing a blunt, awkward object, because the hammer is held through a handle and accelerated rotationally over several turns.

## How speed scales with mass

The best practical description is **between constant impulse and constant energy**, varying with the movement:

- **Constant kinetic energy:**  
  \[
  v \propto m^{-1/2}.
  \]
  This predicts that doubling mass reduces speed by about 29%. It is broadly compatible with shot/hammer distance scaling and is a useful first approximation for trained throwing movements.[4]

- **Constant impulse:**  
  \[
  v \propto m^{-1}.
  \]
  This would make speed fall much faster with mass. It may be more relevant to a brief push or collision-like action, but it does not fit the medicine-ball findings well across modest load changes, where heavier balls retained substantial momentum.[1][8]

- **Real human throws:**  
  A power-limited model is often more realistic:
  \[
  P=\tfrac12 m v^2/t,
  \]
  so if movement duration changes with load, the exponent need not be exactly \(-1/2\). Heavy objects also force a different technique and reduce the distance over which force can be applied.

Using a light-object reference around 10 m/s at 4 kg and an approximate \(m^{-1/2}\) law gives roughly 3.7 m/s at 30 kg, 2.6 m/s at 60 kg, and 1.5 m/s at 180 kg. The lower ranges in the table account for the much worse grip, bulk, deformability, and limited acceleration path of crates, tyres, and hay bales. Thus, for these objects, **roughly constant energy is the closest simple rule, but practical release speed will usually decline somewhat faster than \(m^{-1/2}\) once the load becomes too heavy or awkward**.

> Follow-up question: How far does a standing adult move or fall after being struck in the chest by a 30 kg box at about 5-15 m/s, and by a 4 kg object at the same speeds? Cite crash-test dummy, pedestrian-impact or biomechanics impact data (displacement of the body or the centre of mass, and whether the person falls).

There is **no defensible single distance** from the stated masses and speeds alone. The result depends mainly on the box’s stopping time, contact location and height, friction, posture, whether the person steps, and how much of the impact becomes rotation rather than whole-body translation. Pedestrian-impact research describes chest impacts as producing both translation and rotation, with kinematics determined by the contact-force location relative to the body’s centre of mass.[1]

## Idealized upper-bound estimate

Assume:

- Adult mass: \(75\ \mathrm{kg}\)
- The object stops and transfers all its linear momentum to the adult
- Impact is approximately horizontal
- No impulse is lost to deformation, rebound, or the person’s stepping response

The adult’s initial velocity would be approximately

\[
v_{\text{adult}}=\frac{m_{\text{object}}v_{\text{object}}}{75\ \mathrm{kg}}.
\]

| Object | Speed | Ideal adult velocity | Ideal displacement during a 0.5-s free/coasting interval |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| \(4\ \mathrm{kg}\) | \(5\ \mathrm{m/s}\) | \(0.27\ \mathrm{m/s}\) | \(0.13\ \mathrm{m}\) |
| \(4\ \mathrm{kg}\) | \(15\ \mathrm{m/s}\) | \(0.80\ \mathrm{m/s}\) | \(0.40\ \mathrm{m}\) |
| \(30\ \mathrm{kg}\) | \(5\ \mathrm{m/s}\) | \(2.0\ \mathrm{m/s}\) | \(1.0\ \mathrm{m}\) |
| \(30\ \mathrm{kg}\) | \(15\ \mathrm{m/s}\) | \(6.0\ \mathrm{m/s}\) | \(3.0\ \mathrm{m}\) |

These are **not predicted walking distances**. A standing adult remains supported by the floor, so friction and corrective stepping rapidly change the motion. The actual whole-body centre-of-mass translation is usually much smaller than the free-flight estimate unless the impact knocks the person off balance.

## Whether the person falls

A chest strike can cause a fall when it moves the body’s centre of mass beyond the support area faster than balance reactions can recover it. Biomechanics defines loss of balance in those terms: the vertical projection of the centre of mass leaves the base of support.[3] A study specifically examining backward falls after an external force to the chest reports that people can fall backward after such an impact, while conscious adults bend and use reflexes that reduce fall speed compared with a rigid dummy.[5]

A rough interpretation for a normally braced adult is therefore:

- **4-kg object at 5 m/s:** likely a noticeable shove, torso motion, or one recovery step; a fall is unlikely unless the person is already unstable, surprised, has a narrow stance, or the object catches high on the chest.
- **4-kg object at 15 m/s:** possible loss of balance and backward step/fall; the outcome is highly posture-dependent.
- **30-kg object at 5 m/s:** substantial impulse; a backward step, rotation, or fall is plausible, especially with a high chest impact and feet fixed.
- **30-kg object at 15 m/s:** extremely severe impact; a fall is highly plausible, with potentially several metres of subsequent sliding or tumbling—but the initial centre-of-mass displacement before ground contact still cannot be inferred from mass and speed alone.

Crash-test dummies are useful for chest compression and injury measurements, but they are poor direct predictors of how a living adult will recover balance: pedestrian-impact analyses note that dummies do not bend during a fall, whereas humans reflexively bend and damp the motion.[5] Vehicle-pedestrian impacts also involve distinct contact, flight, and ground-sliding phases, so post-impact travel can greatly exceed the body’s initial movement at the moment of chest contact.[7]

For scale, the object momenta are \(20\)–\(60\ \mathrm{kg\,m/s}\) for the \(4\)-kg object and \(150\)–\(450\ \mathrm{kg\,m/s}\) for the \(30\)-kg object. The corresponding kinetic energies are \(50\)–\(450\ \mathrm{J}\) and \(375\)–\(3375\ \mathrm{J}\), respectively. Those energy values indicate injury potential, but they do not determine displacement because displacement depends on impact duration, deformation, rotation, and the floor’s reaction.
