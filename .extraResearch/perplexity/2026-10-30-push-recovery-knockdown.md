> Question: Human balance and fall biomechanics, with numbers and sources: what impulse or momentum (N·s) applied to a standing adult (70-90 kg) knocks them off their feet, versus what they can recover from by stepping? Give push-recovery thresholds from perturbation studies (center-of-mass velocity change in m/s that triggers a step vs a fall; e.g. Hof's extrapolated center of mass, waist-pull and shoulder-push experiments), and how this depends on where the impulse lands (head/shoulder vs chest vs legs) and whether the person is braced. Then estimate: an 8 kg object at 3, 5 and 10 m/s, a 4.5 kg sheet-metal panel at 4 m/s, a 1 kg mirror at 10 m/s, and a 20 kg wheel at 5 and 10 m/s striking the torso: which knock a person over? Separate documented findings from estimates.

A single universal “knockdown impulse” does not exist. Falling depends strongly on **contact height and direction, contact duration, foot friction, posture, anticipation, stepping speed, and whether the person is constrained**. The most defensible comparison is between an applied impulse \(J\) and the horizontal center-of-mass (CoM) velocity it produces:

\[
J=\Delta p\approx M_{\mathrm{body}}\Delta v_{\mathrm{CoM}}
\]

Impulse equals change in momentum.[7]

## What perturbation studies imply

Hof’s extrapolated-CoM framework defines

\[
x_{\mathrm{CoM}}^{*}=x_{\mathrm{CoM}}+\frac{v_{\mathrm{CoM}}}{\omega_0},
\qquad
\omega_0=\sqrt{\frac{g}{l}}
\]

where \(l\) is effective leg length. Dynamic balance requires the extrapolated CoM to remain within the available base of support; ordinary CoM position alone is insufficient.[1][2]

For a typical adult with \(l\approx 1\) m, \(\omega_0\approx3.1\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\). If the CoM starts near the middle of a roughly \(0.4\)-m-long foot base, the theoretical velocity that carries \(x_{\mathrm{CoM}}^{*}\) to a front or rear boundary is approximately:

\[
v_{\mathrm{CoM}}\approx \omega_0(0.2)\approx0.6\ \mathrm{m/s}
\]

That is a **no-step or fixed-foot estimate**, not a universal experimental fall threshold. A person can often recover by rapidly placing a foot beyond the original base, and Hof’s framework explicitly treats stepping as the way to bring the extrapolated CoM back inside the new base.[1][3][4]

Practical interpretation for an initially quiet, unbraced adult:

| CoM velocity immediately after a perturbation | Likely response |
|---:|---|
| \(<0.2\ \mathrm{m/s}\) | Usually ankle/hip correction; stepping may not be necessary |
| \(0.2\)–\(0.4\ \mathrm{m/s}\) | Step likely, especially for lateral or posterior perturbations |
| \(0.4\)–\(0.6\ \mathrm{m/s}\) | Large compensatory step(s) required; recovery becomes unreliable |
| \(>0.6\ \mathrm{m/s}\) | Often outside a fixed-foot stability margin; a rapid, sufficiently large step may still recover |
| \(>0.8\)–\(1.0\ \mathrm{m/s}\) | High probability of a fall unless the person anticipates, has room to step, or catches/supports themselves |

These bands should be treated as **engineering estimates**, not sharply documented universal cutoffs. The sources found establish the extrapolated-CoM criterion and stepping mechanism, but do not support one single experimentally validated velocity separating “step” from “fall” for every adult and perturbation direction.[1][3][4]

For a 70–90 kg person, the corresponding whole-body momentum and impulse estimates are:

| CoM velocity change | 70 kg | 80 kg | 90 kg |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| \(0.2\ \mathrm{m/s}\) | 14 N·s | 16 N·s | 18 N·s |
| \(0.4\ \mathrm{m/s}\) | 28 N·s | 32 N·s | 36 N·s |
| \(0.6\ \mathrm{m/s}\) | 42 N·s | 48 N·s | 54 N·s |
| \(0.8\ \mathrm{m/s}\) | 56 N·s | 64 N·s | 72 N·s |
| \(1.0\ \mathrm{m/s}\) | 70 N·s | 80 N·s | 90 N·s |

Those values are the **impulse actually transferred to the body’s effective CoM**, not necessarily the incident object’s full momentum.

## Why incident object momentum is not the same as body impulse

For an object,

\[
p=mv
\]

and its maximum available momentum is:

| Object | Momentum |
|---|---:|
| 8 kg at 3 m/s | 24 N·s |
| 8 kg at 5 m/s | 40 N·s |
| 8 kg at 10 m/s | 80 N·s |
| 4.5 kg at 4 m/s | 18 N·s |
| 1 kg at 10 m/s | 10 N·s |
| 20 kg at 5 m/s | 100 N·s |
| 20 kg at 10 m/s | 200 N·s |

Actual transferred impulse is lower or equal to the object’s incoming momentum and depends on restitution, deformation, sliding, rotation, impact location, and whether the object remains in contact. A flexible panel may deliver much less peak impulse than its momentum suggests; a rigid, compact object can deliver a larger fraction over a short interval.

## Estimated torso-impact outcomes

The following are **estimates**, not direct findings from the cited perturbation literature. They assume a standing, initially unbraced adult, a predominantly horizontal torso strike, adequate traction, and no protective catch.

| Object and speed | Available momentum | Approximate interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| 8 kg at 3 m/s | 24 N·s | Often a step or stumble; not reliably a knockdown |
| 8 kg at 5 m/s | 40 N·s | Can provoke a large step; may knock down if high on the torso, lateral, rearward, or poorly anticipated |
| 8 kg at 10 m/s | 80 N·s | Strong knockdown potential; likely to exceed recoverable limits if a substantial fraction transfers |
| 4.5 kg panel at 4 m/s | 18 N·s | Momentum is modest; likely step/stagger rather than reliable knockdown, especially if the sheet bends or rotates |
| 1 kg mirror at 10 m/s | 10 N·s | Usually insufficient by momentum alone for a reliable whole-body knockdown; injury and sharp-fragment hazards are separate issues |
| 20 kg wheel at 5 m/s | 100 N·s | High knockdown potential; likely dangerous even with incomplete transfer |
| 20 kg wheel at 10 m/s | 200 N·s | Very high knockdown potential and severe injury risk |

For an 80 kg person, if the object transferred its entire listed momentum to the body, the nominal CoM velocity changes would be:

- 8 kg at 3 m/s: \(24/80=0.30\ \mathrm{m/s}\)
- 8 kg at 5 m/s: \(40/80=0.50\ \mathrm{m/s}\)
- 8 kg at 10 m/s: \(80/80=1.00\ \mathrm{m/s}\)
- 4.5 kg at 4 m/s: \(18/80=0.225\ \mathrm{m/s}\)
- 1 kg at 10 m/s: \(10/80=0.125\ \mathrm{m/s}\)
- 20 kg at 5 m/s: \(100/80=1.25\ \mathrm{m/s}\)
- 20 kg at 10 m/s: \(200/80=2.5\ \mathrm{m/s}\)

Real values will generally be lower because only part of the object’s momentum becomes translational CoM momentum.

## Impact location and bracing

**Head or shoulder.** A high strike produces a larger tipping moment about the feet:

\[
\tau \approx Fh
\]

where \(h\) is the vertical distance from the ankle/foot contact region. It can also rotate the trunk and head, making the CoM trajectory harder to control. However, a glancing shoulder impact may rotate the person without delivering the full impulse to the CoM.

**Chest or abdomen.** A central chest impact tends to transfer momentum more directly to the trunk and whole-body CoM. It is often more efficient for translating the body backward or sideways, especially when the feet cannot move quickly.

**Legs.** A low strike can remove or rotate the base of support directly. Its required impulse may be much smaller than a torso strike because it can buckle, sweep, or displace a foot rather than accelerate the entire body. A moving foot or knee can therefore cause a fall with less total whole-body momentum than a chest impact.

**Braced person.** Bracing stiffens the body and may permit a larger external impulse to be transmitted into the ground through the feet without immediate trunk motion. It can improve recovery if friction and posture are favorable, but it can also increase peak forces and make a rigid collision more injurious. If the feet are blocked, the person is against a wall, or stepping is prevented, the recoverable impulse is substantially lower.

**Anticipated versus surprise impact.** Anticipation permits pre-positioning, muscle activation, and earlier stepping; an unexpected impact is more likely to produce a step delay and fall at the same CoM velocity.

## Bottom line

For a freely standing 70–90 kg adult, roughly **30–50 N·s delivered effectively to the torso** is enough to demand a substantial recovery step and may cause a fall in unfavorable conditions. Around **50–80 N·s** has strong knockdown potential, while **100 N·s or more** is generally a severe perturbation. These are not universal thresholds: a low leg strike, blocked feet, high shoulder strike, or poor footing can reduce the required impulse dramatically, whereas an anticipated, braced person with room to step may recover from considerably more.
