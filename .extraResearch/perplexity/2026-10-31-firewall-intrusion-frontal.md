> Question: For a typical modern mid-size sedan (e.g. Toyota Camry, Honda Accord, Ford Fusion/Crown Victoria) in frontal crash tests, give measured numbers with sources: (1) the static gap between the engine block's rear face and the firewall (dash panel), in mm; (2) engine setback (rearward engine block displacement) in full-width rigid-barrier tests at 48 km/h (FMVSS 208), 56 km/h (NHTSA NCAP) and 64 km/h, and in the 64 km/h 40% offset deformable barrier (IIHS/Euro NCAP); (3) occupant-compartment intrusion at those tests, in cm, separately for the toe pan / footwell, brake pedal, instrument panel, lower hinge pillar, upper hinge pillar (A-pillar foot), and the cowl / windshield base, with IIHS rating thresholds for 'good' (e.g. lower hinge pillar <= 15 cm); (4) any published firewall/dash panel load or stiffness the engine meets (kN, kN/m). Say which figures are measured and which are estimates.

No defensible public dataset provides all of those quantities together. In particular, published crash-test reports generally measure **occupant-compartment point movement**, not engine-block displacement, engine–firewall clearance, or firewall force. Therefore, the requested engine figures cannot be reported as measured values without vehicle-specific engineering drawings, pre/post-crash coordinate measurements, or raw test instrumentation.

## What is actually published

| Requested quantity | Publicly measured data status |
|---|---|
| Static engine-block rear face to firewall gap | Generally **not published** in NHTSA, IIHS, or Euro NCAP consumer-test results. It varies substantially with engine, transmission, mounts, exhaust, and model year. |
| Engine setback at 48, 56, or 64 km/h full-width tests | Generally **not published**. Crash videos or photographs cannot establish setback accurately because the engine and body deform in three dimensions and the reference coordinate system is absent. |
| Engine setback in 64 km/h, 40% offset deformable-barrier tests | Generally **not published** as an engine measurement. |
| Firewall/dash-panel load or stiffness | Generally **not published** for production sedans. A stiffness value would require a defined load case, boundary conditions, deformation point, and force–displacement curve; “firewall stiffness” is not a single standardized vehicle-test quantity. |
| Occupant-compartment intrusion | **Measured and published**, especially by IIHS. |

IIHS records the movement of specified interior points, including three toepan points, the footrest, brake pedal, lower instrument panel, upper dash, and hinge-pillar locations.[10] Its measurement protocol defines the hinge-pillar reference locations and records their three-dimensional coordinates before and after the crash.[1]

## Representative measured IIHS values

These are actual published measurements from IIHS frontal moderate-overlap tests—not estimates of engine movement:

| Vehicle/test result | Lower hinge pillar | Footrest | Toepan | Brake pedal | Upper hinge pillar | Upper dash / instrument panel |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2020 Kia Optima, driver side | 14 cm | 7 cm | 4 cm | 3 cm | 11 cm | upper dash 11 cm; lower IP 9 cm[3] |
| 2018 Nissan Altima, driver side | 6 cm | 6 cm | 3 cm | published in test table | 1 cm | upper dash 3 cm; lower IP 3 cm[5] |
| 2025 Lexus IS, driver side | 7 cm | 12 cm | 5 cm | 7 cm | 3 cm | upper dash 4 cm; lower IP 4 cm[4] |

The Kia Optima result also reports a maximum lower-hinge-pillar intrusion of 14 cm, a left-toepan value of 4 cm, and a brake-pedal value of 3 cm.[3] These values are occupant-compartment coordinate changes, not necessarily direct inward displacement along the vehicle longitudinal axis; IIHS uses resultant three-dimensional movement for several structural evaluations.[10]

## “Good” thresholds

The example threshold in the question is approximately correct for IIHS structural assessment: a **lower hinge-pillar maximum of 15 cm or less** is within the strongest structural-performance range. However, IIHS does not reduce the entire rating to one universal limit for every point. The structural evaluation considers multiple points and deformation patterns, including the footrest, toepan, brake pedal, instrument panel, upper and lower hinge pillar, and pillar movement.[10]

A useful comparison is the 2020 Kia Optima: its 14 cm lower-hinge-pillar movement is just within that example limit, while the 2025 Lexus IS recorded 7 cm.[3][4] The 2018 Nissan Altima recorded 6 cm in the cited test.[5]

## Why the requested speed-by-speed table cannot be filled reliably

FMVSS 208 at 48 km/h, NHTSA NCAP at 56 km/h, and 64 km/h full-width or offset tests are different test configurations. Even if pre- and post-crash photographs show the engine bay, they do not provide:

- the original and final engine-block reference coordinates;
- engine rotation and translation separately;
- crush of the front structure versus movement of the firewall;
- powertrain-mount deformation;
- the engine’s contact force and contact area against the dash panel.

Consequently, assigning values such as “engine setback = 80 mm” or “firewall load = 300 kN” from consumer crash-test results would be an **estimate**, not a measured test result. The available sources support measured occupant-compartment intrusion only; they do not support numerical engine setback, static clearance, or firewall load for the Camry, Accord, Fusion, or Crown Victoria. NHTSA does maintain engineering crash-test databases containing reports, instrumentation, photographs, and videos, but a vehicle- and test-specific report would have to be identified and examined individually.[9]

**Bottom line:** the IIHS intrusion figures above are measured; the requested engine setback, engine–firewall gap, and firewall force/stiffness figures are not established by the cited public crash-test results and should not be presented as measured numbers.
