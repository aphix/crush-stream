> Question: In full-width rigid-barrier frontal crash tests of passenger cars at 48 km/h (FMVSS 208/212 windshield retention), 56 km/h (NHTSA NCAP) and 64 km/h, how often does the laminated windshield crack, and from what cause: A-pillar/cowl deformation of the windshield aperture, the passenger airbag deploying against it, occupant head contact, or the hood/engine being pushed into the cowl? Give any measured aperture distortion (mm) or diagonal change at which laminated glass cracks, and at what crash speed windshields typically stay intact in a rigid-wall test. Cite sources; say which figures are measured and which are estimates.

There is **no defensible published frequency or universal crack threshold** for laminated windshields at 48, 56, or 64 km/h. The available regulatory and NHTSA material supports a narrower conclusion: **windshield retention is measured, but glass cracking itself is not a standardized pass/fail outcome**, and published crash reports often record retention without identifying whether a crack occurred.

## What causes the cracking?

Among the proposed causes, the most technically plausible primary cause is **local deformation of the windshield aperture—especially the A-pillars, header, and cowl—combined with bending and edge loading of the laminated glass**.

- **Passenger-airbag contact:** The airbag can push the dummy’s head into the windshield; NHTSA test material explicitly describes the head contacting the windshield through the airbag.[2][7] That can produce local glass damage, but it does not establish that airbag contact is the usual cause of cracking.
- **Occupant head contact:** Head contact can crack or locally fracture the laminate, but it is an event-dependent secondary mechanism rather than a predictable speed threshold. FMVSS 208 procedures specifically account for possible dummy-head/windshield contact.[4][8]
- **Hood/engine intrusion:** This is not normally the initiating mechanism in a full-width rigid-barrier test unless there is severe rearward powertrain or hood intrusion. The available sources do not support treating it as the typical cause.
- **A-pillar/cowl deformation:** This is the best-supported structural explanation, but the searched NHTSA reports do **not** provide a general measured aperture-distortion value at which ordinary laminated glass cracks.

## Speeds and observed outcomes

| Test speed | What can be stated reliably |
|---|---|
| **48 km/h** | FMVSS 212 is a windshield-retention test at speeds up to and including 48 km/h against a fixed barrier.[10] The requirement concerns retaining a specified fraction of the windshield perimeter—not remaining crack-free. Passive-restraint vehicles must retain at least 50% on each side; other vehicles must retain at least 75%.[9][10] |
| **56 km/h** | NHTSA NCAP full-frontal rigid-barrier reports show windshields can remain fully retained. A 56.0-km/h Nissan Armada test reported 100% retention and no protected-zone intrusion, with pre- and post-test perimeter measurements unchanged.[6] Another test at 56.53 km/h likewise reported 100% retention.[1] These reports do not establish that the glass was entirely crack-free. |
| **64 km/h** | No source retrieved here provides a reliable population frequency, aperture-distortion threshold, or representative “typical” crack rate at 64 km/h. A 64-km/h rigid-wall impact generally imposes substantially greater body and glass loading than the regulatory 48-km/h test, but the result remains vehicle- and impact-condition-specific. |

## Measured versus estimated figures

**Measured figures:**

- FMVSS 212 test speed: **up to 48 km/h**.[10]
- NCAP examples: **56.0 km/h** and **56.53 km/h**.[1][6]
- Example NCAP windshield retention: **100%**, with unchanged measured perimeter—4,678 mm before and after in one report, and 4,398 mm before and after in another.[1][6]
- Regulatory retention limits: **50% per side** for passive-restraint vehicles and **75% total** for vehicles without passive restraints.[9][10]

**Not established by the retrieved sources:**

- A measured aperture-distortion or diagonal-change value in millimetres at which laminated glass cracks.
- A measured crack probability at 48, 56, or 64 km/h.
- A reliable general statement that windshields “typically” crack at one of those speeds.
- A verified speed at which windshields typically remain intact in all rigid-wall tests.

Thus, any numerical claim such as “the windshield cracks after \(X\) mm of aperture distortion” or “cracking occurs in \(Y\%\) of 56-km/h tests” would be an **estimate or unsupported extrapolation**, not a result established by the cited NHTSA material. The strongest evidence available indicates that windshields can remain fully retained—and in reported cases retain their measured perimeter unchanged—at approximately 56 km/h.[1][6]
