> Question: What is the 'Sentient World Simulation' (SWS)? Who built it and for whom, what is publicly documented about its architecture, how it models individual people and groups, what it calls its simulated entities, what data drives it, and what results or evaluations have been published? Point to primary sources (papers, patents, official reports, the original press coverage) rather than commentary.

The **Sentient World Simulation (SWS)** was a proposed, large-scale, continuously updated **agent-based model of the real world**. Its 2006 concept paper described the aim as creating a “synthetic mirror of the real world” calibrated with current information so that alternative policies, events, and courses of action could be explored before—or instead of—testing them in reality.[2]

## Who built it, and for whom?

The principal developer was **Purdue University’s Synthetic Environment for Analysis and Simulation (SEAS) group**, led by Purdue professor **Alok Chaturvedi**. The concept paper lists researchers from Purdue and affiliated organizations, including Rashmi Chaturvedi, Brian Armstrong, Dan Dolk, and Paul Drnevich.[5]

Public documentation describes SWS as intended primarily for **U.S. defense and homeland-security analysis**. Contemporary reporting said Chaturvedi was presenting the project to **DARPA** and discussing it with the **Department of Homeland Security**; the reporting also characterized the project as being developed for military and government use rather than as a commercial product.[3] Purdue’s faculty résumé records DARPA support in connection with SWS-related work.[9]

The public record does **not** establish that a fully operational, planet-scale system was deployed as often claimed online. It documents a concept, a research platform, demonstrations and related military applications, but not an independently verified system modeling every person on Earth in real time.

## Publicly documented architecture

The core platform was **SEAS—Synthetic Environment for Analysis and Simulation**—an agent-based simulation environment intended to support multiple models and competing theories rather than impose one fixed behavioral theory.[2]

The SWS concept paper describes several architectural elements:

- A **synthetic environment** representing political, military, economic, social, informational, and infrastructure domains—the **PMESII** framework.[1][2]
- A scalable mechanism for integrating models operating at different **spatial and temporal resolutions**.[7]
- A “society of simulations” approach in which multiple component models could interact.[1]
- Continuous calibration against incoming real-world information rather than a one-time simulation.[2]
- A representation spanning individuals, organizations, institutions, infrastructure, geography, and larger social or national aggregates.[2]

The architecture was therefore less a single predictive algorithm than a **federation of models and data feeds** hosted in an agent-based environment. The public papers do not provide a complete software specification, source code, system diagram with implementation details, or reproducible benchmark suite.

## What entities does it simulate?

The concept paper’s basic building blocks include:

- **Individuals**
- **Organizations**
- **Institutions**
- **Information**
- **Geographies**[7]

It also describes infrastructure and the interactions among entities and their environments.[2] A later paper describes the system as containing more than **12 million intelligent agents** representing populations, organizations, institutions, and leaders across more than 60 countries.[6] That figure should be treated as a claim in the later publication, not as independent evidence that SWS achieved global, real-time coverage.

The published material generally calls these simulated entities **agents** or **intelligent agents**. The system’s modeling levels range from individual people through groups, communities, nations, and the synthetic world as a whole.[1][2]

## How individual people and groups are modeled

The public description is primarily conceptual. Individuals are modeled as agents whose behavior emerges from:

- Their attributes and roles
- Their relationships with other agents
- Their institutional and geographic context
- Rules or behavioral models
- Information and environmental conditions
- Interactions with organizations, infrastructure, and other populations

Groups are represented through organizations, institutions, communities, and larger social or national structures. The system is intended to capture both **entity behavior** and **emergent trends** arising from interactions among entities and between entities and their environment.[2]

The papers do not publicly document a validated, person-specific psychological model for every individual. Nor do they establish that the system maintained a unique digital twin of every real person. References to “billions of agents” or detailed modeling of individuals should therefore be distinguished from the more limited technical descriptions in the primary papers.

## What data drives it?

The 2006 concept paper identifies several categories of input for continuous calibration:

- Major events
- Opinion polls
- Demographic statistics
- Economic reports
- Trend shifts
- Publicly available news and web information
- Classified intelligence, in the proposed architecture[1][2]

The paper also refers to **eXtensible Net Assessment (XNA)** as a mechanism for incorporating assessment information, including classified intelligence, into the synthetic environment.[1]

These sources were intended to update model states and parameters, not necessarily to provide a literal record of every person’s activity. Public documentation does not specify a complete operational data pipeline, individual-level database schema, classification boundaries, or the accuracy and latency of the proposed feeds.

## Results and evaluations

The publicly available record contains **descriptions, demonstrations, and methodological claims**, but little in the way of independently reproducible evaluation.

A later paper reports that SWS had more than 12 million agents and had been used as an ultra-large-scale, multi-agent model for “whole of government” analysis.[5][6] Contemporary coverage reported that the project was intended to generate alternative futures and examine interactions among multiple sides.[3]

However, the public primary material does not appear to provide:

- A validated forecast-accuracy study
- Comparisons against baseline forecasting methods
- A peer-reviewed evaluation of individual or group behavioral accuracy
- Error rates, calibration curves, or out-of-sample prediction results
- A reproducible public implementation
- Evidence that the system reliably predicted real-world events at global scale

The strongest defensible conclusion is that SWS was a **government-oriented research and analysis program built around Purdue’s SEAS platform**, designed to integrate heterogeneous data and agent-based models across PMESII domains. Its publicly documented architecture and ambitions are substantial, but claims that it became a fully operational, all-seeing simulation of humanity go beyond what the primary public record demonstrates.

The most useful primary sources are the **2006 SWS concept paper** for the architecture, entities, data, and intended use; the later academic paper **“Got a problem? Agent-based modeling becomes mainstream”** for reported scale and implementation claims; Purdue’s faculty records for institutional and funding context; and the 2007 contemporary report for the original public description and DARPA/DHS context.[2][3][6][9]
