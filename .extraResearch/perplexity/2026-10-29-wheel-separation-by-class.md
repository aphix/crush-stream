> Question: Vehicle crash engineering, numbers with sources. For each of these car types: compact hatchback (e.g. VW Golf), mid-size sedan (Toyota Camry), rear-drive muscle coupe (Ford Mustang GT), mid-size SUV (Toyota RAV4/Highlander), full-size pickup (Ford F-150), and a 1980s-style body-on-frame police sedan (Ford Crown Victoria): (1) what front suspension does it use (MacPherson strut, double wishbone, solid axle) and what are typical ultimate failure loads in kN of the lower ball joint, lower control arm, steering knuckle, tie rod and wheel hub/stud set; (2) in small-overlap or offset frontal crash tests (IIHS, NHTSA, Euro NCAP), how far (cm) is the front wheel pushed rearward/inward before the suspension separates or the wheel detaches, and at what longitudinal crush of the front rail; (3) in a slow quasi-static crush (car crusher, compactor, or static roof/door crush rigs) does the wheel tear off once the crushing face passes the wheel centre, or does it fold in with the structure; (4) rear wheel attachment strength vs front (multi-link, torsion beam, live axle). Give engineering-order estimates where exact data is unpublished, and say which.

There is no defensible published database that reports all requested quantities—especially component ultimate loads, wheel displacement in centimeters, or front-rail crush at suspension separation—by vehicle type. Crash-test reports usually provide photographs, injury measures, and qualitative structural observations, not those measurements. The numerical values below are therefore **engineering-order estimates**, not test-certified values.

IIHS small-overlap tests use a 64 km/h impact with approximately 25% frontal overlap.[6][7] These impacts primarily load the wheel, suspension, hinge pillar, and outboard structure; the wheel may be displaced or sheared away while the main longitudinal rail remains comparatively undeformed.[5][7]

## Estimated front suspension and component loads

Values are approximate **single-component ultimate loads** in the dominant load direction. They represent likely failure ranges for production parts, not guaranteed design limits. A crash can fail a mounting bracket, bolt, bushing, weld, or knuckle before the named component itself reaches these loads.

| Vehicle type | Typical front suspension | Lower ball joint | Lower control arm | Steering knuckle | Tie rod / end | Wheel hub and stud set |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Compact hatchback, Golf class | MacPherson strut; lower arm and one lower ball joint | 25–50 kN | 35–70 kN | 50–100 kN | 15–30 kN | 35–70 kN |
| Mid-size sedan, Camry class | MacPherson strut; lower arm and ball joint | 30–60 kN | 45–85 kN | 60–120 kN | 18–35 kN | 45–85 kN |
| Rear-drive muscle coupe, Mustang GT class | MacPherson strut front; lower control arm/ball joint | 35–70 kN | 55–100 kN | 70–140 kN | 20–40 kN | 55–100 kN |
| Mid-size SUV, RAV4/Highlander class | MacPherson strut front | 35–75 kN | 55–110 kN | 70–150 kN | 20–40 kN | 55–110 kN |
| Full-size pickup, F-150 class | Independent double wishbone front; upper and lower ball joints | 50–100 kN | 80–160 kN | 100–220 kN | 25–50 kN | 80–160 kN |
| 1980s-style body-on-frame police sedan, Crown Victoria class | Unequal-length double wishbone front | 45–90 kN | 70–140 kN | 90–180 kN | 25–50 kN | 70–140 kN |

The ball-joint values are consistent with the fact that suspension joints can experience several times static wheel load during severe impacts; one published technical summary cites roughly 3.5 times static wheel load for pothole-type loading and more than 10 kN as a typical axial-strength scale for passenger-car joints.[2] Those figures should not be confused with crash ultimate loads.

For a MacPherson strut, the “lower control arm” is usually the principal lateral and longitudinal load path, while the strut carries much of the vertical and damper load. For a double-wishbone pickup or large sedan, the lower ball joint and lower arm generally carry higher vertical and impact loads because of greater mass, tire radius, and suspension leverage.

## Estimated wheel movement in offset frontal impacts

The following ranges are estimates for the struck-side wheel center relative to the body, measured after the initial impact and before complete suspension separation. They are not standardized IIHS, NHTSA, or Euro NCAP output values.

| Vehicle type | Wheel rearward movement | Wheel inward/lateral movement | Approximate front-rail crush when wheel/suspension is lost |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Compact hatchback | 15–40 cm | 5–20 cm | 20–50 cm |
| Mid-size sedan | 20–45 cm | 5–25 cm | 25–55 cm |
| Mustang-class coupe | 20–50 cm | 5–25 cm | 25–60 cm |
| Mid-size SUV | 20–50 cm | 5–30 cm | 25–65 cm |
| Full-size pickup | 15–40 cm | 5–25 cm | 30–75 cm |
| Crown Victoria-class sedan | 20–55 cm | 5–25 cm | 30–70 cm |

These ranges vary more with barrier geometry, overlap percentage, impact angle, tire contact, and whether the wheel catches the barrier than with vehicle marketing category. At small overlap, the wheel can be driven rearward into the toe-board, A-pillar, or rocker area even when the main front rail has little direct crush. Research on small-overlap loading specifically identifies the wheel and suspension as common load paths and reports cases where the wheel was sheared away while the longitudinal member remained visually undeformed.[5]

“Wheel detachment” also needs definition. The tire and wheel may separate from the hub; the hub may separate from the knuckle; the knuckle may separate from the control arms; or the entire suspension corner may tear out of the body. These produce very different apparent displacements.

## What happens in slow quasi-static crushing?

A car crusher does not reproduce an offset crash. It applies a broad, slow, highly constrained load, so the answer depends on which part of the vehicle is being crushed and whether the wheel is restrained.

| Vehicle type | Likely behavior when the crushing face reaches wheel-center height |
|---|---|
| Compact hatchback | The wheel usually folds inward or rearward with the fender and lower structure; localized hub or knuckle failure can occur if the face directly traps the tire. |
| Mid-size sedan | Similar behavior; the wheel often becomes a stiff local obstacle and redirects load into the rocker, floor, and suspension mounts. |
| Muscle coupe | The front corner tends to fold rearward/inward, with possible lower-arm or ball-joint separation before hub failure. |
| Mid-size SUV | The taller tire and stronger suspension can retain the wheel longer, then force a larger local fold or suspension-mount tear-out. |
| Full-size pickup | The wheel, tire, and control-arm system are relatively robust; the frame and suspension brackets may deform before the hub/stud set fails. |
| Crown Victoria-style sedan | The body-on-frame front corner commonly transmits load through the frame, control arms, and crossmember; the wheel may fold or remain attached while brackets and frame sections deform. |

Thus, the general answer is **neither universally “tears off” nor universally “folds in.”** If the crusher face contacts the tire broadly, the wheel often folds with the surrounding structure. If the tire is trapped against a rigid edge or the wheel is loaded laterally, the lower ball joint, control-arm mount, knuckle, or hub can fail and the wheel can detach. Static crushing is particularly poor for predicting dynamic wheel separation because inertia, tire compliance, impact pulse, and barrier contact geometry are absent.

## Rear-wheel attachment compared with front

| Rear suspension type | Relative attachment strength | Typical failure behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Torsion beam, common in compact hatchbacks | Generally simpler and robust in vertical loading; less compliant in lateral crash deformation | Beam bushings, trailing-arm mounts, or axle deformation usually precede wheel detachment |
| Independent multi-link, sedans/SUVs | Several links distribute loads, but each link and knuckle can be bent or pulled from its mount | Wheel may toe sharply, move inward, or remain attached through one or more links |
| Live axle, Crown Victoria and many trucks | Very strong vertical and longitudinal load path; both wheels are coupled through the axle housing | Tire, wheel, half-shaft, spring mount, or axle bracket can fail, but the axle often remains structurally connected |
| Independent rear suspension in Mustang/F-150 variants | Strong knuckle and subframe system, but more individual links and mounts provide more possible failure locations | Local link or knuckle separation is more likely than a complete axle-style assembly departure |

The rear corner is often **stronger as a complete load path** than the front because it lacks steering articulation and, in a live axle, couples both wheels through a rigid housing. The front nevertheless has larger crash vulnerability because it must accommodate steering, driveshafts, engine packaging, and crush space. A rear wheel can still detach early if a lateral impact loads the wheel rim, trailing arm, knuckle, or axle end directly.

## Bottom line

- **MacPherson-strut cars:** lower-arm and ball-joint failure are common suspension-corner failure paths; estimated ultimate loads are roughly 25–75 kN for the joint and 35–110 kN for the arm.
- **Double-wishbone trucks and large sedans:** stronger and heavier components, typically about 45–100 kN at the lower ball joint and 70–160 kN at the lower arm.
- **Offset frontal wheel movement:** a practical estimate is roughly 15–55 cm rearward and 5–30 cm inward, but published crash protocols generally do not report these dimensions.
- **Front-rail crush:** suspension loss can occur with approximately 20–75 cm of local crush, but the wheel may separate while the main rail remains relatively intact.
- **Quasi-static crushing:** broad crushing usually makes the wheel fold or redirect load into the structure; concentrated trapping is what most readily produces wheel tear-off.
- **Rear attachment:** live axles are generally strongest as complete assemblies, multi-link systems distribute loads but have more individual failure points, and torsion beams usually deform through their beam or body mounts rather than losing a wheel outright.

The component-load, wheel-displacement, and rail-crush numbers above are explicitly **engineering estimates**. The cited crash literature supports the impact conditions and qualitative load paths, but not the complete numerical table.[5][6][7]
