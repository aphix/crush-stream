> Question: For sliding contact between two painted steel car panels, how do contact pressure and sliding speed or distance change which paint layer is exposed (clear coat only, base coat, primer or e-coat, bare steel)? Give typical scratch and scrape line widths (mm to cm) for light, moderate and heavy sideswipes, and say whether a deeper scrape is mostly wider or only deeper.

Contact pressure primarily controls **damage depth**, while sliding distance and speed control how long the contact persists and how much paint is removed or transferred. The exact result depends strongly on edge sharpness, debris, panel curvature, paint condition, and whether the opposing surface is painted or metallic.

## What becomes exposed

| Contact condition | Likely visible result |
|---|---|
| Very light pressure, clean smooth contact | Clear coat only: gloss loss, haze, or fine marring; often polishable |
| Light-to-moderate pressure | Clear coat is breached locally; base-coat color or transferred paint may appear |
| Moderate pressure or a hard edge | Base coat is cut through in narrow tracks; primer may become visible |
| High pressure, sharp/protruding contact, or trapped grit | Primer and sometimes e-coat are removed; steel may show at the deepest points |
| Severe scrape with panel deformation | Broad areas can lose all paint layers, exposing bare steel and possibly leaving gouges |

Typical automotive layer thicknesses are roughly **45 µm clear coat, 10–20 µm base coat, 25–40 µm primer, and about 17–22 µm e-coat**; the total paint system is commonly around 0.1 mm, excluding the metal substrate.[1][3][5][10] Thus, a scratch only tens of micrometres deep can expose the base coat or primer, while bare steel generally requires removal of most or all of the coating stack.

## Effect of pressure, speed, and distance

- **Pressure:** Higher normal force increases penetration and plastic deformation. A broad, rounded contact may create a wide shallow rub; a sharp edge or grit concentrates force into a narrow, deep line.
- **Sliding distance:** More distance usually increases abrasion, heat, paint transfer, and the length of the mark. It can also widen a mark if the contact repeatedly ploughs loose coating away, but distance alone does not necessarily make it deeper.
- **Sliding speed:** Speed mainly changes frictional heating, viscoelastic response, transfer, and the likelihood that debris is dragged through the coating. At ordinary vehicle sideswipe speeds, pressure and edge geometry usually dominate depth; speed is not a reliable standalone predictor of which layer is exposed.
- **Changing pressure during the event:** A sideswipe can begin as clear-coat scuffing and deepen later if the panels load together or an edge catches. Long horizontal paint scratches are characteristic of sideswipes, and changing depth along the trail can indicate changing lateral pressure.[6][9]

## Approximate visible line widths

These are practical order-of-magnitude ranges, not forensic limits. “Width” means the visible affected band or dominant scratch track, not necessarily the microscopic cutting width.

| Sideswipe severity | Typical line or scrape width | Common appearance |
|---|---:|---|
| Light | **0.1–2 mm** for individual fine lines; a haze or collection of marks may form a **1–10 mm** band | Clear-coat scuffing, marring, or paint transfer |
| Moderate | **2–10 mm**, occasionally up to **20 mm** where edges or debris drag | Local base-coat loss, narrow primer exposure, multiple parallel tracks |
| Heavy | **10–50 mm** commonly; severe contact can produce **5–10 cm** or more of irregular scraped band | Primer/e-coat loss, bare steel at grooves, gouging, and possible denting |

A **deeper scrape is usually not simply wider**. Depth is governed mostly by pressure concentration, edge radius, hardness, and trapped particles; width is governed more by contact geometry, panel overlap, motion path, and how much coating is laterally removed. A sharp object can make a sub-millimetre groove through to steel, whereas a broad rounded panel can remove paint over centimetres while leaving a relatively shallow depression. Multiple adjacent grooves can make the total damaged band wider without each groove being deeper.

Because paint transfer can overlay the original damage, apparent color or layer exposure should be interpreted cautiously: microscopy or cross-sectional inspection is needed to distinguish clear-coat loss, base-coat exposure, transferred paint, primer/e-coat exposure, and bare steel. Modern automotive systems place clear coat over base coat, primer, and e-coat on the metal substrate.[1][5][7]
