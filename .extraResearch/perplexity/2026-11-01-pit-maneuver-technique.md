> Question: Explain exactly how the PIT (Precision Immobilization Technique / Pursuit Intervention Technique) maneuver is taught to police drivers, step by step, and why each step matters physically. Specifics: matching the suspect's speed before contact, the contact point (police front bumper/fender against the suspect's rear quarter panel, behind the rear wheel), the offset and overlap in feet or inches, the steering input (a gentle steer into the suspect car, not a slam) and how far to continue steering, why it rotates the suspect car (yaw moment about its centre of mass, the rear tires losing lateral grip), the follow-through (accelerating through the contact, then braking/straightening away so the police car is not caught in the spin), the ideal speed window (many agencies 35-40 mph, why higher speeds risk rollover), positioning of backup units to block after the spin ('pin' or 'box' after PIT), what goes wrong (contact too far forward, too much speed difference, over-steering), and why PIT is preferred over ramming/T-boning (controlled rotation, lower energy, predictable spin direction, officer safety). Cite police training sources (FLETC, state academies, IACP, published agency policies, trainer articles, physics analyses).

A PIT is a **trained, policy-controlled vehicle-intervention technique**, not a generic “ram.” Exact geometry, speed limits, and authorization rules vary by agency; many policies restrict routine PIT to roughly **35–40 mph**, while FLETC student instruction commonly uses about **25–35 mph**. Higher-speed PIT may be treated as deadly force or allowed only under exceptional circumstances.[1][2][3]

## What the maneuver is intended to do

The police vehicle applies controlled lateral force to the fleeing vehicle’s **rear quarter**, causing the fleeing vehicle to yaw—rotate about its vertical axis—until it loses forward alignment and stops. The objective is controlled immobilization, not crushing or penetrating the other vehicle.

### Training sequence

1. **Assess whether PIT is authorized and justified.**  
   The officer considers the seriousness and immediacy of the threat, traffic, pedestrians, road width, intersections, slopes, barriers, vehicle type, and escape path. Policies commonly require balancing the danger of continuing the pursuit against the danger created by PIT. Agencies also commonly restrict PIT near cliffs, crowds, opposing traffic, and other rollover hazards.[1][3][9]

2. **Match the fleeing vehicle’s speed.**  
   The pursuing driver closes the gap without creating a large speed differential, normally approaching at approximately the same speed. Matching speed matters because a large differential converts the contact into a high-energy collision, increases the chance of the police car riding up or sliding off, and makes the resulting trajectory less predictable.

3. **Move into the offset position.**  
   Training generally places the police vehicle alongside or slightly behind the target, with the **front corner—often the bumper or front fender—aligned against the target’s rear quarter panel, just behind the rear wheel**. The purpose is to act on the portion of the target that can be rotated rather than the rigid, central body structure.

   There is no universal, defensible “exact number of inches” for overlap. Published policies generally describe the contact area rather than prescribing a single measurement, because bumper shapes, wheelbases, lane width, vehicle loading, and training-car geometry differ. A claim that every agency teaches a fixed offset such as exactly one or two feet would overstate the consistency of police curricula.

4. **Apply gentle steering into the target.**  
   The officer uses a deliberate steering input toward the fleeing vehicle—not an uncontrolled swerve or a full-force T-bone. The contact is maintained briefly and progressively. Training emphasizes keeping enough throttle or forward drive to prevent the police vehicle’s bumper from sliding off, while avoiding a violent shove.

5. **Why the target rotates.**  
   The contact force acts laterally at a point behind the target’s center of mass. Because the force is offset from that center, it creates a yaw moment:

   \[
   \tau = rF
   \]

   where \(F\) is the lateral contact force and \(r\) is the distance from the target’s center of mass to the contact line. The rear tires are pushed sideways beyond their available lateral grip, so they slide; the front tires continue guiding the vehicle’s original motion. That combination produces rotation, commonly approaching a 180-degree spin. FLETC materials explicitly describe yaw as rotation about the vehicle’s vertical axis, while agency descriptions identify the rear-quarter contact as the source of the predictable spin.[1][2]

6. **Continue the steering only long enough to establish rotation.**  
   The officer does not keep steering deeply into the target throughout the entire spin. Once the target has begun rotating, excessive steering can turn a controlled intervention into a prolonged side impact, cause the police vehicle to follow the target into the spin, or produce an unpredictable trajectory. The precise release point is taught through practical exercises and depends on speed, traction, contact quality, and available roadway.

7. **Follow through and separate.**  
   The police driver maintains controlled forward drive during contact so the front corner stays engaged, then reduces contact, straightens the steering, and brakes as necessary while moving away from the target’s rotation. This “follow-through” is important because abruptly stopping at contact can leave the police vehicle entangled with the spinning car. Separation keeps the patrol vehicle from being pulled into the target’s path and preserves room for braking or evasive action.

8. **Avoid the target’s final path.**  
   The target may spin toward the shoulder, median, another lane, or a barrier. The officer must anticipate that path rather than simply stare at the contact point. A successful PIT is not necessarily a perfect 180-degree rotation; the relevant result is that the fleeing vehicle loses effective forward control and can be safely contained.

9. **Backup units contain the stopped vehicle.**  
   Pursuing units should not blindly enter the spin. Once the target has rotated or stopped, backup officers can position to prevent escape—sometimes described operationally as a **pin**, **box**, or containment. The precise arrangement is agency-specific, but the principle is to use multiple vehicles and safe angles to block likely escape routes while leaving officers a protected approach path. This is a coordinated post-PIT action, not a license for uncontrolled secondary impacts.

## Speed window and rollover risk

The often-cited routine window of **35–40 mph or below** reflects the sharp increase in consequences as speed rises, not a universal law of vehicle dynamics. FLETC’s student lesson speeds are reported around **25 and 35 mph**, and Illinois State Police policy generally places PIT at **40 mph or less**; another published policy prohibits PIT above **35 mph** except where deadly force is justified.[1][2][3]

At higher speeds:

- Kinetic energy rises with the square of speed, \(E_k=\tfrac12 mv^2\).
- A lateral displacement can send the target into curbs, medians, poles, opposing traffic, or pedestrians.
- A vehicle with a high center of gravity can trip on a curb, soft shoulder, or wheel load and roll.
- The police vehicle also faces greater risk of losing control or colliding with the target after the spin.

These risks explain why some policies classify higher-speed PIT as potentially deadly force and why training demonstrations at speeds above ordinary student practice are generally limited to instructors or exceptional circumstances.[2][3][9]

## Common errors

- **Contact too far forward:** Striking near the target’s door or front half produces a more direct side collision, gives less yaw leverage, and increases occupant-injury and vehicle-entanglement risk.
- **Contact too far rearward or glancing:** The bumper may slide off, producing little rotation and potentially sending the police vehicle toward the shoulder.
- **Too much speed difference:** The impact energy and closing angle become excessive, making the result resemble a ram rather than a controlled intervention.
- **Over-steering:** A large or prolonged steering input can pull the police car into the target, destabilize both vehicles, or cause the officer to follow the spin.
- **Insufficient forward drive:** The patrol car may lose contact before enough yaw is created.
- **Ignoring the environment:** A technically successful spin can still be dangerous beside a drop-off, fixed object, opposing lane, crowd, or rollover-prone shoulder.
- **Poor coordination:** Backup units entering too early can strike either vehicle or remove the escape space needed for the primary officer to recover.

## Why PIT is preferred to ramming or T-boning

When properly authorized and performed within policy, PIT is preferred because it applies force at a deliberate rear-quarter location, creates a more predictable yaw direction, and seeks to stop the target through rotation rather than a high-energy frontal or perpendicular collision. A T-bone transfers substantial force directly into the side of the target vehicle and its occupants; a conventional ram can involve larger closing speeds and less predictable trajectories. PIT is still dangerous—especially at speed, against tall vehicles, or near hazards—and is not inherently “safe.” Its advantage is **relative control and predictability**, not the absence of crash forces.[1][2][5]

Only officers who have completed agency-approved training should attempt it, and the controlling agency policy—not a generic diagram or informal driving advice—determines the permitted speed, geometry, force classification, and follow-up procedure.[1][3]
