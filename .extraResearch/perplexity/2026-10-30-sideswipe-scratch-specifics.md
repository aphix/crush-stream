> Question: In real car-to-car sideswipes, at what lateral closing or pressing speed (m/s), or contact force (kN), does the damage go from a clear-coat scuff or paint transfer to primer showing, and then to bare steel? Give accident-reconstruction or insurance figures (low-speed sideswipe studies, SAE papers on side-scrape damage, crash reconstruction of sideswipe marks) in m/s or kN.

There is **no validated accident-reconstruction threshold** in m/s or kN at which a sideswipe predictably changes from clear-coat scuffing to primer exposure or bare steel. Damage depends strongly on paint-system hardness and thickness, panel curvature, contact geometry, grit or debris, relative yaw, intrusion, and how long the surfaces remain in contact. Published reconstruction guidance generally treats paint transfer and scrape depth as **qualitative evidence**, not force gauges. Sideswipes are predominantly tangential, friction-dominated events and commonly produce elongated scrapes with little crush or low vehicle delta-\(V\).[1][2]

### Practical engineering interpretation

| Observed result | What can reasonably be inferred | Defensible speed/force figure |
|---|---|---|
| Clear-coat scuff or polishing | Light normal contact with sliding; the surfaces did not substantially cut through the coating | **No universal threshold** |
| Base-coat or primer showing | Greater local pressure, abrasive asperities, panel-edge contact, or contamination; often caused by concentrated contact rather than high vehicle speed | **No universal threshold** |
| Bare steel | Localized cutting, severe abrasion, exposed edge, pre-existing damage, or substantial intrusion; not reliably attributable to a single closing speed | **No universal threshold** |

A **lateral closing speed** is also not the same as scraping severity. Two vehicles can have a substantial longitudinal speed difference but almost zero lateral closing speed if they travel nearly parallel. Conversely, a small lateral drift can create a deep mark if a sharp bumper edge, mirror mount, wheel opening, fastener, or dirt particle concentrates the load. The search results support the general reconstruction point that nearly horizontal marks indicate nearly equal travel speeds, while mark angle and changing depth can help establish relative motion and increasing contact pressure—but they do not provide numerical paint-damage thresholds.[5]

### Why kN thresholds are not transferable

Contact force is controlled by local stiffness and geometry. A broad door-to-door contact may spread load over a large area and leave only transfer, while a narrow protrusion can exceed coating failure stress at a much lower total force. A simple estimate,

\[
p=\frac{F}{A},
\]

shows why total force \(F\) cannot be assigned a universal threshold without knowing the real contact area \(A\). Frictional scraping is likewise governed approximately by

\[
F_t=\mu F_n,
\]

where \(F_n\) is normal force and \(\mu\) varies with paint, plastic, metal, dust, and moisture.

Accordingly, figures such as “primer at \(x\) m/s” or “bare steel at \(y\) kN” should not be presented as insurance or SAE-established limits. The available results describe sideswipe mechanics and paint-transfer interpretation but contain **no substantiated SAE or accident-reconstruction table** giving those thresholds.[1][2]

For a defensible claim or reconstruction, the useful measurements are the mark’s height, direction, width, layer sequence, paint-transfer direction, panel deformation, protrusion geometry, and any pre-existing corrosion or chips. A qualified examiner can sometimes infer relative motion and whether contact pressure increased along the mark, but cannot reliably back-calculate a unique m/s or kN value from coating depth alone.[5]

> Follow-up question: A typical moving-car sideswipe on a painted steel flank: how wide is the whole scraped band, how many separate parallel scratch lines does it contain, and how far apart are they (mm)? How does that change between a light brush, a firm sideswipe and a hard grinding sideswipe? Cite collision-repair, forensic or reconstruction sources and photos descriptions.

There is **no defensible published “typical” number** for sideswipe-band width, scratch-line count, or spacing. The available collision and forensic sources describe pattern interpretation—parallel marks, paint transfer, direction, and microscopic striations—but do not establish standardized millimetre dimensions for light, firm, or grinding contacts.[1][5][6][7]

A practical **scene-documentation estimate**, not a validated forensic norm, would be:

| Contact severity | Whole visible scraped band | Distinct parallel lines visible to the eye | Approximate spacing |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Light brush | ~5–30 mm | 1–5 intermittent lines | ~1–8 mm |
| Firm sideswipe | ~20–100 mm | 3–15 lines, often discontinuous | ~1–10 mm |
| Hard grinding sideswipe | ~50–200+ mm | Many overlapping grooves; commonly >10, often not countable | ~0.2–5 mm for fine striations, with larger gouge spacing superimposed |

These ranges should **not be presented as reconstruction measurements** unless a photograph includes a scale and the surface is examined directly. A light brush may leave only a transfer smear or a few faint lines; a severe contact can remove clearcoat and paint across a broad, irregular zone, making “number of lines” dependent on lighting, camera resolution, panel curvature, paint colour, and the contacting object.

The most reliable qualitative progression is:

- **Light brush:** superficial transfer or scuffing, short or faint parallel marks, little exposed substrate.
- **Firm sideswipe:** continuous longitudinal abrasion, clearer striations, paint transfer, and possible localized deformation.
- **Hard grinding sideswipe:** broad paint removal, deep gouges, displaced metal or plastic, embedded foreign paint, and overlapping striation families.

Collision-damage guidance identifies continuous parallel scrapes as evidence of gradual lateral contact and recommends close photographs of the damage, including a ruler or coin for scale.[1][6] Forensic paint guidance likewise treats transferred paint and the underlying damaged surface as evidence to be photographed, sampled, and compared—not as a pattern from which generic line counts can be inferred.[5][7][9] The fine lines inside a gouge are called **striations**; they can indicate relative movement direction, but they are microscopic or near-microscopic features rather than a fixed set of widely spaced visible scratches.[6]

For a reconstruction, report measurements this way: **maximum band width**, **length**, **orientation**, **number of clearly resolved grooves in a specified measured segment**, **mean groove spacing**, and **uncertainty**. For example: “72 mm maximum abrasion width; 8 resolvable longitudinal grooves over a 100-mm segment; mean spacing 6.4 mm; additional sub-millimetre striations unresolved.” That is more scientifically supportable than labeling a mark “typical” for a particular impact severity.

> Follow-up question: For two cars sliding along each other in a sideswipe, does the scrape mark run the full relative slide distance, or is it broken or dashed? What is a typical gap and length pattern, and do the marks show direction (e.g. a feathered start and a heavier end, or the reverse), as used in accident reconstruction to tell which way the cars moved? Cite forensic and reconstruction sources.

A sideswipe scrape **does not necessarily run continuously for the entire relative-slide distance**. It may be a long, nearly continuous smear, but it can also appear as **separate patches, short striations, transferred paint areas, gouges, or alternating contact/no-contact segments**. The physical contact exists only while particular protrusions, trim, panels, or deformed parts remain engaged; body curvature, suspension motion, panel flexing, changing yaw, and intermittent separation can create gaps.

There is **no reliable universal “typical gap and length” pattern**. A gap may be only a few millimetres to several centimetres at the component level, while individual marks may range from short streaks to marks extending across several panels. The observed pattern depends on vehicle geometry, relative yaw and lateral velocity, contact pressure, surface material, and whether the vehicles remain pressed together. A reconstruction should therefore document each mark’s position, height, width, depth, continuity, and transferred material rather than infer a fixed spacing rule.

## What the marks can indicate

- **Paint-transfer smears and shallow abrasions** generally indicate sliding contact, but their beginning and end may not equal the beginning and end of the vehicles’ overall relative motion.
- **Gouges, displaced plastic, and metal flow** can provide stronger directional evidence. Plastic trim may be scraped and displaced in the direction of contact, while sheet-metal “pocketing” and panel deformation can show how one surface loaded the other.[7]
- **A feathered or tapered end can be informative**, but it is not automatically the start or finish of motion. A mark may begin lightly and become heavier as contact pressure increases, or begin heavily and fade as the vehicles separate. The interpretation depends on which vehicle’s surface is being examined and on the local geometry.
- **Mirrors, bumper covers, wheel displacement, and panel-gap changes** may show force direction. A mirror pushed forward, a forward-bent bumper cover, or forward-displaced wheel components are indicators of forward force; rearward displacement suggests rearward force.[5]
- The two cars should be analyzed together. Matching paint, corresponding heights, complementary damage, material transfer, and deformation patterns are more dependable than the apparent taper of one scratch alone.[5][7]

For a same-direction sideswipe, a long horizontal band commonly reflects a small relative heading angle and sustained contact. If the cars have different speeds or yaw angles, marks may angle across the panels; the angle and changing depth can help establish relative motion, but they do not by themselves prove which vehicle made the lane change.[4][6]

Thus, a **heavier-to-lighter** or **lighter-to-heavier** progression can support a direction-of-loading opinion, but it should not be treated as a standalone “arrow.” The most defensible reconstruction combines both vehicles’ damage, component geometry, material-flow direction, force indicators, roadway evidence, and final positions. DJS Associates likewise cautions that directionality is not always apparent from damage alone and recommends combining damage analysis with paint matching, tire marks, and rest positions.[5]
