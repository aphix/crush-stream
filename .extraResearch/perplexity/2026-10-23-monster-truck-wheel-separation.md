> Question: Monster Jam truck crashes: how often does a wheel come off in real Monster Jam crashes and rollovers, and what fails when it does (planetary portal hubs, axle shafts, steering knuckles, 4-link brackets, wheel studs)? Give any design strength figures: axle shaft or planetary hub ratings, wheel stud count and grade, beadlock design, and the impact loads (kN or g) these parts are built for.

There is **no reliable public database** of Monster Jam crashes, so no defensible percentage exists for “how often a wheel comes off.” Publicly documented examples suggest a complete wheel departure is **uncommon relative to rollovers**, and many apparent “wheel-off” incidents are actually tire/bead failures, broken wheel restraints, or video-game effects rather than a documented failure of the hub or axle.

## What is publicly established

- Monster Jam trucks use four-link suspension, approximately 30 inches of shock travel, 66×43-inch BKT tires, about 23 psi inflation, and roughly **645 lb per tire-and-wheel assembly**.[9]
- Monster-truck axles commonly use **planetary reduction at the wheel hub**, historically based on heavy military or commercial axles.[3]
- Monster Jam’s public material describes rollovers and the required safety “pull the pin” shutdown, but does not publish crash-frequency statistics or component failure rates.[1][2]
- In one reported non-Monster-Jam monster-truck incident, a front wheel broke away after a landing; the six-bolt wheel-retention restraint reportedly sheared, allowing the wheel to leave the arena.[6] That is evidence of a **retention-system failure**, not proof that the planetary hub, axle shaft, knuckle, or studs failed.
- The reported tire/wheel mass was 645 lb. At only \(10g\), stopping that assembly would correspond to an average force of approximately  
  \[
  F = ma \approx 645\ \text{lb}\times 10g \approx 28.7\ \text{kN}
  \]
  but a crash load cannot be inferred from this alone: the force depends on stopping distance, impact angle, tire deformation, rotation, and load sharing.

## What usually fails when a wheel is lost?

Without teardown reports, the failure mode must be treated as case-specific. The likely possibilities are:

- **Wheel-retention or restraint hardware:** bolts, straps, tether brackets, or their attachment points can shear during a side load or landing. The documented six-bolt restraint failure is the clearest public example.[6]
- **Wheel studs or nuts:** possible if the wheel is loosened, overloaded in bending, or the studs are damaged; public Monster Jam sources do not identify stud count, material grade, torque, or proof load.
- **Wheel center or beadlock hardware:** a tire can separate from the bead or the wheel can deform without the entire hub assembly departing. Public sources do not provide Monster Jam beadlock drawings or bolt specifications.
- **Steering knuckle/spindle or hub housing:** a hard lateral landing can create large bending loads, but no public teardown evidence was found tying a particular Monster Jam wheel departure to a knuckle fracture.
- **Axle shaft or planetary hub:** these are plausible failure points under driveline shock or impact, but a broken shaft generally does not by itself explain the wheel flying away; the wheel may remain attached to the hub housing.
- **Four-link brackets or axle-tube mounts:** these can bend or tear and cause suspension collapse, but they are not normally the direct mechanism that releases the wheel.

## Strength figures: what is and is not published

The requested engineering figures—axle-shaft torque or bending ratings, planetary-hub load ratings, wheel-stud count and grade, beadlock construction, and certified crash loads in kN or g—are **not published in the cited Monster Jam material**. A “20-ton planetary hub” listing exists for a Clark component associated with older monster-truck/ Rockwell applications, but it is not evidence of the specification of current Monster Jam trucks and does not provide a validated crash rating.[10]

Consequently, numbers such as “the axle is built for \(X\) kN” or “the studs withstand \(Yg\)” would be speculation unless supplied by the truck manufacturer, component builder, sanctioned inspection report, or a forensic teardown. The only directly supportable load-scale figure here is the approximately **645-lb wheel/tire mass** and the resulting illustrative inertial force above—not a design limit.[9]
