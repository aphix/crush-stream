> Question: Passenger car crash tests: at what speed or load does a road wheel separate from the car in small-overlap, offset barrier or side-pole crashes? What failure loads (kN) do lower ball joints, control arms, steering knuckles and tie rods have on a typical 1500 kg sedan, and how often do wheels detach in IIHS small-overlap tests?

There is **no single crash speed or universal load** at which a road wheel separates. Separation depends on the suspension design, impact direction, wheel position, corrosion or prior damage, and whether the tire/rim or a suspension joint fails first.

## What the crash tests impose

- **IIHS small-overlap frontal:** 40 mph (64.4 km/h), with 25% of the vehicle width striking a rigid barrier.[1][7]
- **Offset or pole crashes:** speeds and overlap vary by test program; a pole test is usually a localized side impact rather than a standardized front-corner load case. The relevant wheel loads are highly vehicle- and geometry-dependent.
- A 1,500 kg car at 64.4 km/h has about \(242\ \text{kJ}\) of translational kinetic energy, but only a fraction reaches one wheel or suspension corner. It is therefore not valid to convert test speed directly into a wheel-separation load.

The IIHS protocol specifies speed, overlap, vehicle mass, instrumentation, and injury/structural measurements—not a prescribed wheel-detachment threshold.[1][6]

## Approximate suspension failure loads

Public crash-test data generally do **not** publish certified ultimate loads for the lower ball joint, control arm, knuckle, or tie rod in each tested vehicle. The following are reasonable **engineering-order estimates for a typical 1,500 kg sedan**, not universal specifications:

| Component | Approximate ultimate axial/tensile load | Typical crash caveat |
|---|---:|---|
| Lower ball joint | **25–60 kN** | Stud pull-out, socket fracture, or housing/knuckle failure may govern |
| Lower control arm | **30–80 kN** | Bending and local tearing often govern rather than pure axial tension |
| Steering knuckle | **50–120 kN equivalent local load** | Usually fails by fracture or deformation at a thin section; load is geometry-dependent |
| Outer tie rod | **15–35 kN** | Tension/compression buckling and ball-stud pull-out are different failure modes |
| Inner tie rod/rack connection | **20–45 kN** | Rack housing and joint geometry can govern |

These ranges should not be used for certification, forensic conclusions, or restraint-system design. A production component’s proof load, yield load, and ultimate crash load can differ substantially, and manufacturer-specific data or destructive testing is required for a defensible value.

## How often do wheels detach in IIHS small-overlap tests?

There is **no published IIHS statistic establishing a wheel-detachment frequency** across its small-overlap test fleet. IIHS rates occupant protection and structural performance; it does not publish wheel separation as a standard pass/fail outcome or maintain a publicly reported detachment-rate denominator.[6][7]

Consequently, the defensible answer is:

- **Frequency: not quantified by IIHS in the cited public test methodology.**
- Wheel or suspension separation appears to be an **uncommon outcome**, but “uncommon” cannot be converted into a reliable percentage without reviewing every test video and inspection report.
- A wheel that becomes visibly displaced is not necessarily “detached”; the hub, knuckle, control arm, tie rod, brake hose, or wheel may fail independently.

A recall investigation illustrates the safety distinction: separation of a ball joint from the steering knuckle can cause wheel separation and loss of mobility, but that is a component-integrity failure—not a standardized crash threshold.[4]
