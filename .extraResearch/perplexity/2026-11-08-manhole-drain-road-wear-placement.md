> Question: On real paved roads and race circuits, how are manhole covers (sewer, utility), storm drain inlets/gutter grates, utility trench patches, asphalt cracks and potholes, and rubber/skid marks laid out along and across the carriageway? Give concrete numbers: sewer manhole spacing along a street (e.g. 90-120 m, at changes in direction, grade and pipe size, at junctions), lateral position (lane centre, between wheel paths, in the gutter), cover diameter (0.6-0.9 m, frame 0.8-1.0 m), storm drain inlet spacing along the kerb (m) and size, trench patch shape and size (long rectangles crossing the road, 0.6-1.5 m wide) and why they run across a lane near the manhole, where cracks occur (wheel paths, joints, edges), where potholes occur, where tyre rubber and skid marks occur on public roads (braking zones before junctions, accelerating from lights, tight corners) and on circuits (the racing line, start grid, braking zones; marbles off line). Also say which of these a racing-game level artist can safely place on a road without looking wrong, and which look wrong when scattered randomly (e.g. a cobblestone square in the middle of asphalt). Cite sources and say what is published versus typical practice.

On real roads, these features are **constrained by drainage, access, vehicle wheel paths, construction methods, and traffic forces**—not scattered uniformly. Exact dimensions vary by jurisdiction, road class, climate, and utility owner; the figures below distinguish published design criteria from common visual practice.

## Sewers and utility manholes

- **Longitudinal spacing:** A practical visual range of **90–120 m** is reasonable for ordinary urban streets, but published limits vary. Examples include about **107 m** for small sewers and **152 m** for larger ones in one drainage standard,[2] while Charlotte specifies maximum sanitary-sewer spacing of about **152 m, 213 m, and 244 m** for increasing pipe-size bands.[3]
- **Required locations:** Put manholes at **junctions**, changes in **direction/alignment**, **grade**, **pipe diameter**, material, or level—not merely at regular intervals.[5][7][9]
- **Position across the carriageway:** Designers generally try to keep covers out of traffic lanes. Where unavoidable, they are often placed in the **parking lane, near the kerb, in the gutter, near a lane boundary, or between normal wheel tracks**.[4] A cover centered exactly in a heavily loaded wheel path is possible, especially on older streets, but is a less desirable placement.
- **Typical cover dimensions:** A circular access cover commonly appears around **0.6–0.9 m diameter**, with a frame roughly **0.8–1.0 m** across. These are typical visual ranges rather than a universal standard; published storm-manhole examples use internal diameters around **1.2–1.8 m**, depending on pipe size.[7]
- **Visual arrangement:** A manhole may be paired with a small rectangular utility cover, a nearby kerb inlet, or a longitudinal and transverse trench patch. Covers often sit slightly proud, depressed, or surrounded by a square/rectangular asphalt repair.

## Storm-drain inlets and gutter grates

- **Where they occur:** Place them in the **gutter or kerb line**, not in the middle of a lane, except for special surface inlets. They occur at **low points**, sag curves, intersections, and along grades where gutter flow would otherwise exceed the permitted spread.[5][10]
- **Spacing:** There is no universal fixed interval because spacing depends on rainfall, catchment area, gutter slope, crossfall, inlet capacity, and allowable water spread. Published guidance gives examples from roughly **90–180 m** between interception points on ordinary streets, with shorter spacing on flat or high-speed roads.[4] One standard allows up to about **213 m** on some continuously graded streets and about **91 m** on major arterials.[10]
- **Closely spaced series:** Where several inlets are used together, a published recommendation gives only **1.8–6.1 m** between successive inlets in some arrangements, allowing bypass flow to return to the kerb.[4]
- **Size and form:** Common visual sizes are a single rectangular grate approximately **0.45–0.75 m wide and 0.6–1.2 m long**, or a larger double grate around **0.6–1.0 m by 1.0–1.5 m**. These are typical ranges, not a universal specification. The supporting catch basin or manhole is often about **1.2 m internal diameter or larger**; a widely used published minimum is **1.2 m** for manholes.[2][5]
- **Orientation:** Kerb-opening inlets run **parallel to the kerb**; grates in the gutter usually have their long dimension parallel or slightly diagonal to the kerb. At a junction, several grates may sit just upstream of the crossing or stop line.

## Utility trench patches

- **Shape:** A utility repair normally appears as a **long rectangle**, often a narrow strip running along the road, or a rectangular patch crossing one or more lanes. It tends to have straight saw-cut edges, though later settlement, cracking, and irregular resurfacing soften the outline.
- **Width:** A patch crossing a lane commonly looks about **0.6–1.5 m wide**. A longitudinal utility trench is often narrower, around **0.3–0.8 m**, but may be widened because of excavation safety, pavement damage, or poor edges.
- **Why a patch crosses a lane near a manhole:** A lateral sewer, water, gas, telecom, or electrical connection runs from the manhole or kerb-side service into the carriageway. It therefore produces a **transverse or diagonal patch** from the manhole toward the opposite side, while a main utility run produces a **longitudinal patch** along the street.
- **Typical layout:** A realistic cluster might contain one manhole in or near a lane, a **0.8–1.2 m-wide transverse patch** leading toward the kerb or property frontage, and a longer narrow patch continuing along the road. Do not make every patch perfectly centered on a lane; utilities often follow property lines, kerbs, and existing corridors.
- **Published versus typical:** Pavement manuals generally define patches by their limited extent and less-than-full-width nature rather than prescribing one universal width.[1] The dimensions above are representative construction and visual ranges, not a single code requirement.

## Asphalt cracks and potholes

- **Wheel-path cracking:** Repeated traffic loading produces fatigue or “alligator” cracking in the **wheel paths**, especially where the pavement is weakened by water, inadequate thickness, or settlement. It is usually two longitudinal bands, one under each wheel track, rather than a random full-width web.
- **Longitudinal cracks:** These occur along **lane joints**, utility trenches, pavement seams, and the edge of a previous widening or repair.
- **Transverse cracks:** These commonly follow construction joints, thermal movement, or a trench crossing the road. A transverse crack often runs across a lane and may connect to longitudinal cracks.
- **Edge cracking:** Put it near the **kerb, pavement edge, shoulder, drainage line, or poorly supported trench edge**. Water and edge loading make those locations credible.
- **Potholes:** They usually develop where cracks or patches allow water in, particularly in **wheel paths**, around poorly compacted utility cuts, at patched manholes, near drainage defects, and at pavement edges. A large random pothole in an otherwise pristine lane is less convincing than a cluster around a failed patch or wet low point.
- **Patch failure:** A realistic sequence is: rectangular repair → seam cracking → settlement or depression → broken edges → pothole. Pavement guidance treats repaired holes as patches and unrepaired holes as pavement distress rather than as arbitrary surface decoration.[1]

## Rubber and skid marks

### Public roads

- **Braking:** Concentrate dark rubber or skid marks **before junctions, stop lines, crossings, toll points, sharp bends, and traffic lights**, usually in the wheel paths and aligned with the lane.
- **Acceleration:** Put shorter, darker marks **just downstream of traffic lights, stop lines, merge points, or steep starts**, especially where vehicles launch hard.
- **Corners:** On tight bends, rubber appears near the **outside exit**, at the apex, or where vehicles brake and then turn. It is generally curved or follows the lane trajectory rather than forming isolated random blobs.
- **Ordinary traffic:** Public-road marks are usually intermittent and localized. A continuous black racing line through every bend would look more like a circuit than a normal street.

### Race circuits

- **Racing line:** The strongest rubber commonly follows the **repeated racing line**—outside approach, late apex, and outside exit—rather than the geometric center of the pavement.
- **Braking zones:** Put heavy, straight or slightly wavering marks before corners, beginning near braking boards and intensifying toward the turn-in point.
- **Start grid:** Grid boxes, launch marks, and rubber buildup occur along the starting slots and the initial acceleration path.
- **Corner exits:** Add darker acceleration marks where cars put power down, especially near kerbs and on low-grip or heavily used exits.
- **Marbles:** Small rubber debris accumulates **off the racing line**, especially at the outside of corners, on the dirty side of the track, and near the edge of the usable surface. It should be granular and scattered, not a painted stripe.
- **Wet-race caveat:** A dry rubbered-in line can become visibly different from the surrounding asphalt, while standing water may produce cleaner-looking or darker tyre paths depending on surface and lighting.

## What a racing-game artist can place safely

### Usually convincing

- Manholes near lane boundaries, gutters, parking lanes, or between wheel paths.
- A few covers at roughly **90–120 m** intervals, with extra ones at junctions, bends, grade changes, and utility transitions.
- Kerb-side storm grates at low points, intersections, and plausible intervals of roughly **30–150 m**, adjusted to the road slope and drainage story.
- Long rectangular utility patches, especially **0.6–1.5 m** wide when crossing a lane.
- Transverse patches connecting a manhole to a kerb, service corridor, or property side.
- Cracks along wheel paths, lane joints, patch edges, kerbs, and drainage depressions.
- Potholes near failed patches, low points, wheel paths, and road edges.
- Braking and acceleration rubber in traffic-conflict areas; concentrated racing-line rubber on circuits.

### Usually looks wrong

- Manholes or grates scattered at equal random distances without regard to kerbs, junctions, drainage lows, or pipe changes.
- A storm grate in the center of a lane with no drainage or structural reason.
- Many identical covers in the same lane, all perfectly aligned and evenly spaced.
- Utility patches that are tiny decorative rectangles with no connection to a cover, kerb, building frontage, or service route.
- Cracks distributed uniformly like camouflage rather than following wheel paths, joints, edges, and repairs.
- Potholes randomly placed on an otherwise undamaged road, especially clean circular holes with no surrounding cracking.
- Skid marks everywhere, including lane centers on straight roads with no braking or acceleration event.
- A **cobblestone square in the middle of asphalt** unless it represents a deliberate utility reinstatement, historic patch, speed-table material change, or construction detail. Without such a transition, it reads as an asset-placement error rather than a road feature.

The numerical spacing and location rules are published engineering practices, but the visual ranges for cover diameter, patch width, grate dimensions, and rubber distribution are best treated as **typical practice for believable scene construction**, not universal requirements.
