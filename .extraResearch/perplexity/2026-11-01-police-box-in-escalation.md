> Question: When police vehicles have boxed in or surrounded a suspect vehicle at low speed (a 'box-in', 'vehicle containment' or 'pinning' maneuver) and the suspect driver starts trying to force their way out (revving, ramming police cars, reversing, wedging through gaps), what do US police vehicle-pursuit and containment policies and trainers say officers should do? Cover: whether officers escalate to deliberately pushing or pinning the suspect car with their own vehicles (pinning maneuver, intentional contact at low speed), at what speeds intentional contact is allowed (e.g. pinning under ~35 mph / 56 km/h), how officers protect themselves (keeping distance, angling cars, not standing between vehicles), whether ramming at high speed is treated as deadly force, and how quickly they respond once the suspect starts moving. Also: the PIT maneuver's usual allowed speed limit in agency policies (e.g. 35-40 mph). Cite agency policies, IACP or PERF guidance, and training sources; say which are published policy and which are general practice.

US policies generally distinguish **containment/pinning**, **PIT**, and **ramming**—but terminology and thresholds vary by agency. A driver trying to break out is treated as a rapidly escalating event: officers should use trained vehicle tactics, create distance and angles where possible, and immediately reassess whether the suspect’s movement creates a deadly-force threat.

## What published policies generally say

- **Containment/blocking:** Agencies describe containment as positioning police vehicles to prevent escape and avoid initiating a pursuit. Fargo, for example, defines blocking or pinning as a slow-speed, coordinated maneuver intended to contain a stopped or parked suspect vehicle, generally for serious offenders or drivers posing an imminent public-safety threat.[2]

- **Pinning or “pinching”:** Some policies authorize trained officers to make **low-speed, intentional, non-striking contact** to restrict movement. The DOJ policy example calls this “pinching,” a preplanned containment tactic using low-speed contact to render a vehicle immobile; it limits the tactic to trained specialized units with command approval.[5] Other policies similarly define pinning as contact intended to restrict movement without significant damage.[4][7]

- **A moving suspect changes the analysis:** St. Paul’s published policy defines pinning as contact when the suspect vehicle is stationary and expressly distinguishes it from ramming.[1] Thus, once the suspect begins driving, officers should not automatically treat continued forceful contact as ordinary “pinning.” They must use only the trained tactic and force level authorized by their agency, or shift to a pursuit-intervention or deadly-force analysis.

- **Intentional contact at approximately 35 mph:** There is no nationwide rule that universally authorizes pinning below 35 mph. The commonly cited **35–40 mph figures usually concern PIT or agency-specific vehicle-contact rules**, not a blanket permission to ram. Some departments permit PIT at or below 35 mph, while the DOJ policy example permits PIT as intermediate force at 40 mph or below.[3][5] A policy that allows low-speed pinning may still require the suspect vehicle to be stationary or nearly stationary, trained personnel, a clear purpose, and minimal-impact contact.

- **Ramming:** Published policies commonly classify deliberate high-speed vehicle impact as **deadly force** or a high-level force option. Fargo defines ramming as deliberately impacting a vehicle to damage or force it to stop and treats it as deadly force, authorized only when deadly force is justified and no reasonable alternative exists.[2] St. Paul likewise says ramming at higher speeds requires compelling, exigent circumstances and is not authorized unless the deadly-force threshold is met.[1] The DOJ policy states that ramming is prohibited unless a deadly-force situation can be clearly articulated.[5]

## What officers should do when the suspect forces an escape

General training principles—not a single nationwide policy—typically emphasize:

- **Do not stand in the gap or between vehicles.** Officers should remain out of crush zones and avoid positioning themselves where a sudden reverse, lurch, or vehicle-to-vehicle compression can trap them.
- **Maintain reactionary space when feasible.** A police vehicle should not be so close that the officer has no room to brake, steer, or disengage if the suspect moves.
- **Angle vehicles rather than presenting a flat, exposed front or side.** Angling can reduce the chance of being directly struck and provide an exit path, although the exact positioning is agency- and training-specific.
- **Use vehicle containment only by trained personnel.** Fargo expressly limits these tactics to officers who have received department-approved training.[2] Policies also warn that pinning places officers close to the suspect and requires extreme caution.[7]
- **Respond immediately once movement begins.** Officers should not wait for a suspect to gain substantial speed or repeatedly ram police vehicles. They should promptly brake, steer away, reposition, disengage, or apply an authorized intervention tactic, while communicating the movement and reassessing the threat.
- **Escalate force based on the threat, not merely resistance.** A suspect trying to move a vehicle is not automatically subject to deadly force; the key question is whether the vehicle is being used in a manner that creates an imminent threat of death or serious bodily injury to officers or others.

These protective practices are best described as **general police-vehicle-training principles**, unless an agency’s written policy specifically states them. The cited policies clearly establish training requirements, caution around pinning, and the distinction between low-speed containment and ramming, but they do not create one uniform national rule requiring a particular distance or angle.[1][2][4][7]

## PIT speed limits

PIT thresholds vary, but **35–40 mph is a common policy boundary**:

- Albuquerque’s PIT policy prohibits PIT above 35 mph unless deadly force is justified.[3]
- Albuquerque’s pursuit policy treats PIT above 35 mph as deadly force.[10]
- The DOJ policy example permits PIT at 40 mph or below as intermediate force and treats PIT above 40 mph as deadly force.[5]

Even below the stated threshold, PIT can be deadly force when circumstances create a substantial risk of death or serious injury—for example, use against motorcycles, vehicles likely to roll over, vehicles with deflated tires, crowded areas, or dangerous roadside conditions.[5]

## Bottom line

Officers generally may use a **trained, low-speed containment or pinning technique** when their policy authorizes it, but that is not an unlimited license to push a moving car. Once the suspect begins forcing an escape, officers should immediately protect themselves by avoiding crush zones, maintaining maneuvering space, repositioning or disengaging where possible, and using only an authorized intervention tactic. **High-speed ramming is ordinarily treated as deadly force**, while PIT commonly has a 35- or 40-mph policy limit, subject to agency-specific rules and the actual risk of death or serious injury.[1][2][3][5]
