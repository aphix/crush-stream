## 2. Would a 1.5 kg projectile topple a standing adult?

For an initially stationary **70–100 kg** person, the idealized momentum transfer is:

\[
p=mv
\]

| Projectile speed | Momentum | Kinetic energy | Whole-body speed if perfectly sticking |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 30 m/s | 45 N·s | 675 J | 0.64 m/s at 70 kg; 0.45 m/s at 100 kg |
| 70 m/s | 105 N·s | 3,675 J | 1.50 m/s at 70 kg; 1.05 m/s at 100 kg |
| 100 m/s | 150 N·s | 7,500 J | 2.14 m/s at 70 kg; 1.50 m/s at 100 kg |

If the projectile rebounds elastically with approximately the same speed, the impulse can approach \(2mv\), giving twice those idealized whole-body velocity changes. But that is an upper-bound model: torso deformation, projectile deformation, penetration, rotation, foot-ground friction, and recoil distribution usually prevent the entire projectile momentum from becoming forward centre-of-mass motion.

The key distinction is between **post-impact centre-of-mass velocity** and **whether the feet can recover the disturbed posture**. A brief impulse can absolutely produce a centre-of-mass velocity that exceeds a push-recovery boundary; the fact that the force acts for only milliseconds does not invalidate the resulting momentum change. However, a quoted “0.6 m/s no-step” or “about 1 m/s fall” threshold is not a universal knockdown rule: it depends on stance width, foot placement, body posture, direction, friction, reaction timing, compliance, and whether the person can take a step.

### Comparison with less-lethal projectiles

The available less-lethal data show why ordinary bean-bag and 40 mm rounds often injure without reliably throwing a person backward:

- A typical 12-gauge bean-bag round listed in the literature has mass **40 g** and velocity about **82–85 m/s**, giving approximately **3.3–3.4 N·s** of momentum and **134–145 J** of kinetic energy.[3]
- A 37 mm, **40 g** bean bag at **91 m/s** has about **3.64 N·s** of momentum and **166 J**.[3]
- A 40 mm sponge round of **60 g** at **76 m/s** has about **4.56 N·s** and **173 J**; a **27 g** round at **99 m/s** has about **2.67 N·s** and **132 J**.[3]
- A flexible baton round listed at **100 g** and **67 m/s** has roughly **6.7 N·s** of momentum and **160 J**.[2]
- A 40 mm Direct Impact round is listed at **44 g** and **82 m/s**, about **3.6 N·s** and **148 J** in its opened configuration.[2]

Even if all of a 40 g bean-bag projectile’s momentum transferred to a 70–100 kg adult, the ideal whole-body speed change would be only about **0.033–0.057 m/s**. For a 100 g baton round, it would be about **0.067–0.096 m/s**. Those are far below the idealized 0.6–1 m/s whole-body values, explaining why such rounds are generally not mechanically expected to launch or reliably topple a person.

That does **not** mean they are harmless. The cited literature reports severe localized injury, including ocular destruction and skull fracture from a bean-bag round, and describes foam rounds that failed to incapacitate an aggressive person while still producing serious pulmonary contusion.[2] Facial-impact testing also found substantial peak forces and impulses; one listed bean-bag result was **3.1 N·s** impulse with a **1.79 ms** duration.[8]

### Bottom line

- A **1.5 kg** object at **30 m/s** is near the lower edge of the idealized no-step range for a light adult and may or may not topple them.
- At **70 m/s**, the idealized momentum is large enough that a torso strike could plausibly cause a step, loss of balance, or fall, but not with certainty because transfer and posture dominate.
- At **100 m/s**, the momentum and energy are extremely large; a direct torso impact could readily cause a fall, but the principal hazard would be severe blunt trauma, penetration, fractures, or internal injury rather than a clean cinematic “knockback.”
- Standard bean bags, baton rounds, and 40 mm impact rounds usually carry only about **2.5–7 N·s** of momentum, roughly **one to two orders of magnitude below** the 1.5 kg projectile at 100 m/s. They can incapacitate through pain and injury, but their momentum alone is generally too small to reliably knock a standing adult over.[2][3][8]
