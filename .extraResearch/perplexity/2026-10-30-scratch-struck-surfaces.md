> Question: When a car's chrome trim, black unpainted plastic trim, painted plastic bumper cover, rubber tyre or glass is the surface scraped by another car in a sideswipe, what visible damage does each show (scratches, gouges, scuffs, whitening of plastic, no mark), and how visible is it from a few metres away compared with scratched body paint?

In a sideswipe, the visible result depends mainly on **contact pressure, relative hardness, surface texture, and whether material is transferred**. Compared with scratched painted bodywork, marks on trim can be either less noticeable or more visually obvious because they often appear as contrasting white, grey, or shiny lines.

| Contacted surface | Typical visible damage | Visibility from a few metres away |
|---|---|---|
| **Chrome trim** | Fine scratches, bright scuffs, or localized gouges. The chrome may look dull or show a darker substrate if the plating is breached. It may also show transferred paint. | **Often quite visible** if the scratch catches reflected light; fine hairlines may disappear at normal viewing distance. Deep gouges remain conspicuous. |
| **Black unpainted plastic trim** | Grey or white scuffs, whitening, shallow scratches, raised/frayed plastic, and sometimes gouges. The light appearance is commonly from abrasion changing the texture and reflectivity rather than from paint being removed. | **Moderately to highly visible**, especially on textured black trim or in sunlight. A broad pale scuff can be more noticeable than a light scratch in body paint. Deep gouges are obvious. |
| **Painted plastic bumper cover** | Paint transfer, clear-coat scuffs, scratches through the colour coat, or gouges exposing primer or the underlying plastic. A surface scuff may appear as a dull haze without a groove; a deep gouge can show grey/white plastic. [1][2] | **Usually similar to scratched painted metal bodywork** when the paint is removed. A superficial transfer or clear-coat scuff may be less visible and can sometimes be polished out; exposed primer or plastic is plainly visible. |
| **Rubber tyre sidewall** | Usually a dark scuff, smear, abrasion, or shallow gouge. It may leave little visible evidence if the other vehicle slides across the rubber rather than cutting it. Severe contact can remove rubber or create a cut/chunk missing. | **Usually less visible than scratched paint** because the tyre is black and already textured. A fresh grey smear or a deep cut can still be noticeable at close to medium distance. |
| **Glass** | Fine scratches, a cloudy abrasion patch, or a chip/star mark if a hard protrusion strikes it. Ordinary rubbing contact often leaves no mark unless grit or a hard edge is trapped between surfaces. | **Usually inconspicuous from a few metres away** unless the scratch is deep, broad, catches glare, or produces a chip/crack. It may be highly visible from inside the car or at an angle in sunlight. |

### Practical comparison

- **Most likely to show “no mark” or only a faint mark:** tyre rubber, and glass when the contact is smooth and free of grit.
- **Most likely to show whitening:** black unpainted plastic, especially when its surface texture is abraded.
- **Most likely to show a contrasting scuff:** black plastic and chrome.
- **Most likely to show a clearly repairable paint defect:** painted bumper cover, where paint transfer, colour loss, primer exposure, or plastic exposure can be distinguished. [2][3]
- **Most likely to look worse than it technically is:** black plastic, because a shallow texture change can appear as a large pale patch.
- **Most comparable to a scratched body panel:** painted plastic bumper paint; if the scratch reaches through the paint, it is generally as visible as damage on painted metal. [4][5]

A useful rule is that **a mark that does not catch a fingernail is generally a scuff, transfer, or surface abrasion**, while a fingernail-catching line is more likely to be a true scratch or gouge. This is only a visual rule of thumb: clean each surface first, since transferred paint and dirt can greatly exaggerate apparent damage.
