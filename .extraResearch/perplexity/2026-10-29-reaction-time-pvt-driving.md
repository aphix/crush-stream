> Question: Formal human reaction-time numbers from the psychomotor vigilance task (PVT, Dinges and Powell 1985; University of Pennsylvania sleep and chronobiology work by Dinges, Basner, Van Dongen) and from driving studies: (1) PVT mean and median simple reaction time for well-rested healthy adults (ms), fastest 10% of responses, and the lapse threshold (500 ms) and false-start threshold (100 ms); (2) how reaction time and lapses change with sleep deprivation (one night, chronic restriction to 4-6 h), with age (older adults), and with time on task; (3) the 3-minute PVT-B vs 10-minute PVT differences; (4) brake reaction time in driving: expected vs unexpected hazards (Green 2000 'How long does it take to stop?', Olson, AASHTO 2.5 s design value) and how it relates to PVT simple RT; (5) skilled drivers or racers: published simple and choice reaction times for motorsport drivers. Give numbers with their sources and years, and say where a figure is a design value rather than a measured mean.

The most defensible summary is that a well-rested adult’s **standard 10-minute PVT** response is typically around **220–250 ms**, but PVT results are usually reported as response speed \(1/\mathrm{RT}\), median RT, and lapses rather than as a single universal mean. A response of **\(\ge 500\) ms** is conventionally a lapse; a response made before **100 ms** is conventionally a false start.

## 1. Standard PVT reference values

| Measure | Typical interpretation or value |
|---|---:|
| Mean simple RT, healthy rested adults | Approximately **220–250 ms**; about **222 ms** corresponds to a response speed of \(4.51\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) in one reference implementation[1] |
| Median RT | Commonly approximately **210–240 ms** in healthy rested adults; the median is preferred when occasional very slow responses distort the mean |
| Fastest 10% | Roughly **170–190 ms**; this is a descriptive lower-tail value, not a standard diagnostic cutoff |
| Lapse | **\(\ge 500\) ms** on the standard PVT[2][3] |
| False start | Response **<100 ms** after stimulus onset; false starts are responses without a valid stimulus or implausibly early responses |
| Standard test | **10 minutes**, visual stimuli presented at random **2–10 s** intervals[2][4] |

The 500-ms lapse criterion is historical and operational: it does not mean that every 500-ms response represents a complete failure of attention. PVT analyses often use **response speed**, because reciprocal RT is less distorted by the long tail of delayed responses[2].

The approximate mean and median values above should not be treated as universal norms. They vary with hardware latency, instructions, age, test familiarization, sleep history, and whether anticipatory responses are excluded. The frequently cited value of about **222 ms** is an example reference value, not a Dinges–Powell population constant[1].

## 2. Sleep deprivation, age, and time on task

### Acute total sleep deprivation

After one night awake, PVT performance changes in three main ways:

- **Mean and median RT become slower**, usually by tens of milliseconds in group averages.
- **The slow-response tail expands**, producing more responses above 500 ms.
- **Lapses increase substantially**, often more reliably than the mean RT.

The size of the increase is highly variable: some people show modest average slowing but large increases in lapses, while others show marked slowing. Sleep deprivation also produces a smaller but measurable increase in false starts[2][3].

### Chronic restriction to 4–6 hours

Repeated restriction to approximately **4–6 hours of sleep per night** produces cumulative deterioration in vigilant attention. PVT lapses and response speed worsen across successive days, even when participants report only moderate subjective sleepiness; the impairment can resemble, and in some individuals approach, that seen after acute total sleep deprivation[2][4].

A precise universal millisecond increase cannot be assigned because the result depends on baseline sleep need, restriction duration, circadian phase, and whether the outcome is mean RT, median RT, reciprocal speed, or lapse count. **Lapse count and response speed are generally more sensitive endpoints than mean RT**[2].

### Older adults

Older adults generally show:

- Slower average and median PVT responses.
- More variability and a longer upper RT tail.
- More lapses under sleep loss, circadian challenge, or prolonged wakefulness.

However, age effects are not simply a fixed subtraction or addition of milliseconds. Healthy older adults may perform reasonably well when rested, while showing pronounced vulnerability in sustained attention when sleep-deprived. Age, baseline processing speed, medications, and comorbidity all contribute.

### Time on task

Lapses increase progressively during the test. The increase is **not linear in a way that permits simply doubling a 10-minute result**: a 20-minute bout can produce substantially more than twice as many lapses as a 10-minute bout because vigilance failure accumulates with time on task[5].

This is one reason the PVT is useful for fatigue research: it captures both overall slowing and intermittent failures of attention.

## 3. Three-minute PVT-B versus 10-minute PVT

| Feature | Standard PVT | PVT-B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Duration | **10 min** | **3 min** |
| Interstimulus interval | **2–10 s** | Usually **1–4 s** in the original brief design; some implementations use **2–5 s**[2][3][4] |
| Standard lapse threshold | **500 ms** | **355 ms** |
| Sensitivity | Better estimate of stable individual performance and lapse burden | Faster screening test, but fewer trials and fewer observed lapses |
| Typical result | Slower RT and more lapses than PVT-B | Faster RT, fewer lapses, and more false starts than the 10-minute PVT[4] |

PVT-B was designed to preserve sensitivity to total and partial sleep deprivation while reducing administration time. It tracks the standard PVT reasonably well over time, but its values are **not numerically interchangeable** with 10-minute PVT values because it uses a different stimulus schedule and a lower lapse threshold[2][4].

In particular, comparing “lapses” across versions without accounting for the **500-ms versus 355-ms criterion** is misleading. A 3-minute PVT-B also samples less time-on-task fatigue and naturally produces fewer opportunities for lapses.

## 4. Driving brake reaction time

Green’s methodological review gives the following approximate **brake-response times**, measured from the relevant visual event to moving the foot toward the brake:

| Driving situation | Brake reaction time |
|---|---:|
| Fully expected signal, known time and location | **0.70–0.75 s** |
| Unexpected but familiar event, such as lead-car brake lights | About **1.25 s** |
| Surprise hazard, such as an object suddenly entering the path | About **1.5 s** |

Driver expectation can change reaction time by approximately a factor of **2.5**, with additional effects from age, urgency, gender, and cognitive load[6].

These are **brake-response times**, not full stopping times. Total stopping distance additionally includes perception, decision, accelerator-release, brake-application, brake-system buildup, and vehicle deceleration.

The often-used **2.5-s perception–reaction value in highway design** is a **design assumption/value**, not a measured mean for every driver or hazard. It is intentionally conservative and is used for roadway design calculations associated with AASHTO practice. It should not be presented as the typical laboratory brake reaction time.

### Relation to PVT simple RT

A PVT response is a relatively simple, well-practiced stimulus–button response. It mostly measures detection and motor execution under sustained vigilance. Driving requires additional stages:

- Hazard detection and visual search.
- Event interpretation.
- Expectancy and uncertainty.
- Decision to brake.
- Foot transfer and pedal targeting.
- Vehicle and traffic context.

Consequently, a rested PVT RT near **220–250 ms** is not equivalent to a real-world brake reaction time. Even an expected driving signal is commonly around **700 ms**, and unexpected or surprising hazards are around **1.25–1.5 s**[6]. The **2.5-s AASHTO figure is a design value**, not a PVT-equivalent physiological mean.

## 5. Skilled drivers and motorsport racers

Published motorsport studies generally show faster-than-average **choice** and sometimes simple reaction times, but results vary considerably by discipline, apparatus, anticipation, and whether the task is visual, auditory, or sport-specific. A defensible broad range is:

- **Simple RT:** approximately **180–230 ms** in trained drivers/racers.
- **Two-choice RT:** approximately **250–350 ms**.
- More complex or multiple-choice responses: often **350–500+ ms**.

These values should be reported as study-specific means rather than as a universal “racer reaction time.” Training can improve stimulus–response selection and motor execution, but it does not eliminate the additional perception, interpretation, and decision time required for an unexpected road hazard.

The distinction is critical: a racer’s laboratory simple RT may overlap with a healthy adult’s PVT RT, while a racer’s on-track response to an unexpected developing hazard still includes visual scene analysis and action selection. Therefore, it should not be substituted directly for Green’s driving values or for the **2.5-s AASHTO design assumption**.

### Bottom line

- Rested standard-PVT RT: approximately **220–250 ms**, with fastest decile roughly **170–190 ms**.
- Standard PVT lapse: **\(\ge 500\) ms**; false start: **<100 ms**.
- Sleep loss primarily increases **slow responses and lapses**, with progressive impairment under repeated 4–6-hour restriction.
- PVT-B is **3 minutes**, uses a shorter interval schedule and **355-ms** lapse threshold, and is not directly numerically interchangeable with the 10-minute PVT.
- Driving brake response is approximately **0.70–0.75 s when expected**, **1.25 s for an unexpected familiar event**, and **1.5 s for a surprise hazard**[6].
- **AASHTO’s 2.5 s is a roadway-design value, not a measured average reaction time.**
