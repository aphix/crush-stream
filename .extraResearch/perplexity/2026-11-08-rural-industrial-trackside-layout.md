> Question: How are these roadside and site elements laid out in real practice: (a) rural and highway roadside (guardrail offsets, delineator posts spacing, signs, hay bales, fences, roadside trees clear zone from AASHTO Roadside Design Guide, mailboxes, culverts, farm gates), (b) desert roads (cacti, scrub, rock outcrops, drifting sand), (c) industrial yards and ports (shipping container stacking, aisle widths, pallets, drums, jersey barriers, chain-link fences, lighting masts spacing, hazard signage), (d) parking lots (stall dimensions and angles, aisle widths, island trees, cart returns, lamp spacing), (e) motorsport circuits (FIA trackside: marshal posts spacing, tyre walls, catch fencing, advertising boards, grandstand and crowd placement, kerbs, run-off dimensions, start/finish gantries, pit lane) and (f) a Prague/Vienna old-town square and Washington DC rowhouse streets (tree spacing, lamp spacing, planters, cafe zones, kiosks). Give concrete numbers in metres with sources, and say what is published versus an estimate.

The most defensible way to lay these out is to distinguish **published requirements** from **typical planning values**. The numbers below are metric conversions and practical ranges; local highway, fire, port, building, heritage, and FIA/ASN rules always control.

## (a) Rural and highway roadside

### Published or standards-based values

- **Clear zone:** AASHTO does not prescribe one universal width; it varies with speed, traffic, slope, curvature, and roadside hazard. FHWA examples are approximately **2.1–3.0 m** for low-speed, low-volume roads; **9.1–9.8 m** for a straight, level highway at about 97 km/h; and **11.6–14.0 m** for steeper slopes at about 113 km/h. Curves may require up to **50% more**.[6]
- **Vertical-obstruction offset:** FHWA identifies **0.46 m minimum** horizontal clearance to vertical obstructions, unless a design exception applies. This is not equivalent to a full clear zone.[6]
- **Guardrail:** The face of guardrail is normally placed outside the recovery area where practical, but its offset is determined by the barrier system, deflection, slope, drainage, and hazard. A common preliminary allowance is **0.6–1.2 m** from pavement edge or shoulder edge; use the selected system’s tested deflection and agency standard for final design. AASHTO guidance emphasizes that clear-zone width is risk-based rather than a fixed guardrail offset.[2][5][6]
- **Mailboxes:** AASHTO examples allow the mailbox face about **0.15–0.30 m** behind a curb, or **0.20–0.30 m** behind the pavement edge on low-speed residential roads. On very low-volume rural roads, about **2.0 m** from the traveled way may be accepted, with limited cases down to **0.8 m**. At intersections, place it at least **30 m** beyond the intersecting-road centerline, or **60 m** where the intersecting road carries more than 400 vehicles per day.[1]
- **Culverts:** Culvert ends within the clear zone require a traversable end treatment; one state implementation specifies that no part of the treatment protrude more than **0.10 m** above the ground line.[9]
- **Delineator posts:** A common agency practice is **50 m** spacing on tangents and **25 m** on curves, with closer spacing or additional posts at decision points. This is a typical value, not established by the retrieved sources.
- **Signs:** Sign supports should be outside the clear zone where possible, or use breakaway supports. Typical sign-edge setbacks are **1.8–3.0 m** from the pavement edge on high-speed roads, but the controlling value is the road agency’s sign manual.
- **Trees:** New trees should generally be beyond the calculated clear zone unless protected by a tested barrier. A practical rural planning rule is **10–15 m** from the pavement edge on high-speed roads, but this is an estimate rather than an AASHTO universal requirement.
- **Farm gates and field entrances:** Set gates back far enough that a vehicle or tractor can wait completely off the through lane. A typical entrance throat is **10–15 m** long, with gate posts outside the clear zone or shielded; these are design estimates.
- **Hay bales:** Hay bales are not a recognized permanent crash barrier. Place them outside the clear zone, commonly at least **10 m** from the pavement on high-speed roads, and never rely on them as guardrail or terminal protection. This is a safety recommendation, not a universal published setback.

### Practical roadside sequence

From the pavement outward, a typical section is:

1. Shoulder and recoverable graded strip.
2. Drainage swale or traversable ditch.
3. Delineators near the outer edge of the clear zone.
4. Guardrail only where the hazard warrants it, with terminals and transitions set out according to the tested system.
5. Trees, fences, hay storage, farm gates, and utility equipment beyond the clear zone.
6. Culvert crossings treated so that their ends do not create blunt or snagging hazards.

## (b) Desert roads

There is no single AASHTO “desert spacing” table for cacti, scrub, rock, and sand. Layout is generally governed by the same clear-zone and roadside-hazard principles, with additional maintenance and visibility allowances.

- **Cacti and dense scrub:** Treat large, rigid, or tall vegetation as fixed objects. Keep it outside the calculated clear zone; a preliminary landscape setback of **10–15 m** from the pavement edge is commonly sensible on high-speed roads.
- **Low scrub:** Low, sparse vegetation may remain within the clear zone if it does not conceal hazards, obstruct signs, or prevent vehicle recovery. The allowable height and density are agency-specific.
- **Rock outcrops:** Preserve natural rock only when it is outside the recovery area or protected by a designed barrier. A practical preliminary setback is **10–15 m** from the pavement, increasing where the rock face is tall, undercut, or close to the likely vehicle path.
- **Drifting sand:** Keep sand-trapping vegetation and fencing away from drainage and sight triangles. Use broad, maintainable sand-storage areas, typically **15–30 m** or more on the windward side where drifting is severe. This is an estimate; the correct width depends on wind, dune mobility, road geometry, and maintenance capability.
- **Delineation:** In blowing sand, use raised or highly visible delineators at the normal agency spacing, commonly **50 m tangent / 25 m curve**, and consider closer spacing where the roadway edge disappears.
- **Drainage:** Desert culverts and washes can be major hazards despite dry conditions. Treat headwalls and wingwalls as roadside hazards and use traversable treatments or barrier protection.

## (c) Industrial yards and ports

The following are **planning values**, not universal port standards.

| Element | Typical preliminary value |
|---|---:|
| Standard container footprint | **2.44 × 6.06 m** for a 20-foot container; **2.44 × 12.19 m** for a 40-foot container |
| Container stacking aisle | **15–18 m** for common straddle/reach-stacker operations; more may be needed for particular equipment |
| Truck circulation lane | **12–15 m** for two-way heavy-vehicle movement |
| One-way truck lane | **5–7 m** |
| Pallet storage aisle | **3.0–3.6 m** for conventional forklifts; narrower aisles require specialized equipment |
| Drum or packaged-goods aisle | **1.2–2.0 m** pedestrian/material-handling clearance, subject to fire-code storage rules |
| Jersey barrier offset | Commonly **0.3–1.0 m** from an active lane or protected object; final position depends on impact and visibility requirements |
| Chain-link security fence | Typically **2.4–3.0 m** high, often with anti-climb topping |
| Lighting mast spacing | Roughly **40–80 m** for high-mast yard lighting; final spacing comes from photometric calculations |
| Hazard signs | At entrances, pedestrian crossings, tank or drum storage, traffic-control points, and each hazardous-material zone; spacing is determined by sight distance and the applicable code |

Containers should be stacked on engineered pavement with verified load capacity, wind stability, fire access, drainage, and visibility. Drums require segregation, spill containment, compatible storage, and fire separation; aisle dimensions cannot be selected independently of the stored material and fire code.

## (d) Parking lots

Common planning dimensions are:

| Parking arrangement | Stall dimensions | Aisle width |
|---|---:|---:|
| 90-degree parking | **2.5 × 5.0 m** typical; **2.7 × 5.5 m** more comfortable | **6.0–7.5 m** two-way |
| 60-degree parking | About **2.5 × 5.0 m** | **4.5–6.0 m** one-way |
| 45-degree parking | About **2.5 × 5.0 m** | **3.7–4.5 m** one-way |
| Parallel parking | About **2.4 × 6.0 m** | **3.5–4.0 m** lane |

These are typical design values, not a single national standard. Accessible spaces, loading spaces, electric-vehicle equipment, fire lanes, and local parking ordinances can require larger dimensions.

- **Landscape islands:** Commonly **2.5–3.0 m** minimum width for a tree, with **1.5–2.0 m** clear trunk distance from a curb where doors and overhangs must be protected.
- **Tree spacing:** About **8–12 m** on parking-lot rows, depending on mature canopy and utilities.
- **Cart returns:** Typically every **30–60 m** of storefront frontage, or one return serving roughly **20–40 spaces**. This is an operational estimate.
- **Lighting:** Parking-lot poles commonly occur at **25–40 m** spacing for ordinary pole heights, with final spacing determined by pole height, fixture optics, and required illuminance.
- **Pedestrian routes:** Keep accessible routes separate from reversing areas where possible; use islands, bollards, or curbs at crossings.

## (e) Motorsport circuits

FIA requirements are highly site- and category-specific. The FIA circuit drawing requirements define the track, pit-lane, barrier, and related geometry, but do not provide one universal spacing for every marshal post, tyre wall, advertisement, or grandstand.[7] The following should therefore be separated into published principles and estimates.

### Published or FIA-derived principles

- **Pit lane:** In the Formula 1 sporting regulations, the fast lane may be no more than **3.5 m** wide; the pit lane has a fast lane and an inner lane.[3]
- **Marshal-post protection:** FIA karting guidance gives a protection height around marshal posts of **1.0–1.2 m**.[8]
- **Obstruction-free lateral areas:** FIA guidance states that lateral areas up to the first protection should be kept clear of obstructions.[8]
- **Barrier selection:** For shallow impact angles, a continuous smooth vertical barrier is preferred; for larger angles, deceleration and stopping systems such as gravel, foam, tyre systems, or catch fencing are selected according to the hazard.[8]

### Typical circuit-planning values

- **Marshal posts:** Usually one visible post at each major corner, braking zone, incident-prone location, and approximately every **150–300 m** along less complex sections. This is an estimate; the ASN and circuit safety plan determine the actual number.
- **Tyre or energy-absorbing barriers:** Used at likely impact points, not at a uniform interval. A typical barrier run may be **10–30 m** long, extended according to approach speed and impact angle.
- **Catch fencing:** Place behind vehicle barriers where cars could launch toward spectators, workers, or public areas. Typical fencing heights are **3–4 m**, with higher systems where trajectory analysis requires them; these are estimates.
- **Advertising boards:** Commonly **1–3 m** behind the barrier or outside the run-off system, never in a vehicle trajectory or marshal access route.
- **Grandstands and spectators:** Place beyond the primary barrier, catch fence, and safety envelope. A preliminary spectator offset of **10–20 m** beyond the catch fence is common, but certified sightline, debris, and trajectory studies control.
- **Kerbs:** Often about **0.3–0.5 m** wide, with profiles and colors set by circuit and FIA/ASN requirements; avoid abrupt profiles that destabilize vehicles.
- **Run-off:** No universal width applies. A preliminary concept range is **15–30 m** for fast approaches, with larger areas at high-speed corners; final dimensions depend on speed, angle, vehicle type, barrier system, and FIA safety analysis.
- **Start/finish gantry:** Keep support columns outside the track and escape routes; a typical overhead span is about **10–12 m** over a circuit of ordinary width. This is an estimate.
- **Pit lane:** A practical total width is often **10–15 m**, including the fast lane, inner lane, pit boxes, and working clearance, although the retrieved FIA Formula 1 source specifically limits the fast lane to **3.5 m**.[3]

## (f) Prague/Vienna old-town squares and Washington, DC rowhouse streets

Historic districts generally rely on municipal public-realm standards, heritage approvals, accessibility rules, fire access, and street-specific permits. There is no single spacing rule that applies to every Prague, Vienna, or Washington street.

### Typical historic-square arrangement

- **Street trees:** About **6–10 m** apart in formal rows; **8–12 m** is common where mature canopies are large.
- **Lamp posts:** About **20–30 m** apart on ordinary pedestrian streets and **25–40 m** on wider streets, subject to illumination calculations and historic-fixture placement.
- **Planters:** Commonly **1.0–2.0 m** diameter or equivalent rectangular footprint, with at least **1.5 m** clear pedestrian passage beside them.
- **Café zones:** Typically **2–4 m** deep along façades, leaving a continuous pedestrian clear path of about **2.0–3.0 m**; emergency access may require more.
- **Kiosks:** Small kiosks often occupy **8–25 m²**, with about **3–5 m** clear around entrances, corners, and principal pedestrian desire lines.
- **Square furniture:** Keep benches, bollards, planters, and signs aligned to edges or furnishing zones rather than scattered through the central civic space.

### Washington, DC rowhouse streets

- **Street trees:** Commonly **7–10 m** on center, adjusted for driveways, hydrants, underground utilities, and mature canopy.
- **Street lamps:** Usually about **25–35 m** apart on residential streets, with tighter spacing near intersections and corners.
- **Planters:** Often **0.6–1.2 m** wide; preserve at least **1.5 m** of accessible sidewalk clear width, and preferably **1.8–2.4 m** where pedestrian volumes are high.
- **Café seating:** Usually placed in a furnishing or curb zone, often **2–3 m** deep, without reducing the accessible pedestrian route below the applicable public-right-of-way requirement.
- **Kiosks and sidewalk objects:** Keep clear of curb ramps, fire hydrants, utility covers, building entrances, and sight triangles; a practical separation of **1.5–3 m** from these features is common but permit-specific.
- **Rowhouse street section:** A typical block may have a **1.5–2.5 m** furnishing/tree zone, a **1.8–3.0 m** pedestrian clear zone, and curbside parking or loading outside it. These are planning estimates.

### What is actually “published”

- The **AASHTO/FHWA clear-zone concept and mailbox values** are published guidance.[1][2][5][6]
- **Culvert-end treatment requirements** are published in state highway manuals and similar agency standards.[9]
- **FIA pit-lane and marshal-protection principles** are published, but many exact circuit dimensions remain site-specific.[3][7][8]
- The remaining values—delineator intervals, tree setbacks, container aisles, parking-lot lighting, café dimensions, kiosk clearances, and urban furnishing spacing—are **typical preliminary-design estimates**, not universal standards.
- For construction, replace estimates with the governing road agency manual, fire code, port operator standard, parking ordinance, accessibility standard, heritage approval, and FIA/ASN circuit safety dossier.
