> Question: When a monster truck rolls over and lies on its roof or body, where do the wheels sit relative to the chassis: compressed against the bump stops (tires near the body) or hanging at full droop on limit straps? Describe photos or footage of rolled Monster Jam trucks and the wheel position relative to the chassis and body.

Usually, **the wheels are not simply hanging at full droop**. In a typical rolled Monster Jam truck, the suspension is loaded according to how the truck came to rest: wheels on the ground may be pushed **upward toward the chassis and bump stops**, while wheels off the ground may extend outward until the **limit straps or shock travel stop** them.

Monster Jam trucks use four-link suspension with roughly 30 inches of travel and nitrogen-charged shocks.[3] Their limit straps specifically prevent the axles from pulling the shocks apart at full droop.[6] Air bump stops cushion the final portion of upward travel as the axle approaches the chassis.[1]

## What rolled trucks generally look like

- **Truck resting on its roof:** The roof and cage support much of the truck’s weight. The tires often sit visibly away from the body rather than being uniformly crushed against it. Depending on the truck’s attitude and suspension geometry, some corners can be near full droop, with the axle hanging on the shocks and limit straps.
- **Truck resting on its side:** The lower-side tires are usually forced upward toward the chassis and may be close to, or visibly contacting, the bump-stop area. The upper-side tires are unloaded and commonly hang downward, sometimes with a noticeable gap between tire and body.
- **Truck lying partly on its body panels or cage:** The suspension can be asymmetrical. One axle or one side may be compressed while the opposite side droops. The wheels therefore do not normally sit at one common position relative to the chassis.
- **Immediately after a rollover:** Suspension motion can leave the tires oscillating. Once settled, the extended corners are held by shock extension and limit straps—not by the tires “falling” indefinitely.

The photos and rollover footage typically show this **mixed condition**: wheels on the load-bearing side tucked upward toward the chassis, and wheels on the unloaded side hanging outward/downward. A truck lying upside down is therefore not a reliable example of all four wheels being either fully compressed or fully drooped. The exact position depends on which tires, axle, roof structure, and suspension links are carrying the truck’s weight.
